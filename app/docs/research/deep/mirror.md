## 2. Research questions and findings

### Q1. What is the mode, and what may the thesis claim from the bilateral-training and mirror-therapy literature?

- Definition. Cochrane defines simultaneous bilateral training as a motor activity completed by both upper limbs at the same time but independently (Coupar et al. 2010, Methods [FT, PMC page]). Mirror meets it: the same finger is cued on both hands, both hands are visible and there is no mirror (code).
- BATRAC. Four 5-minute bouts per one-hour session, three a week for six weeks, pushing and pulling two T-bar handles in phase or alternately to a metronome at individually set rates of 0.67 to 0.97 Hz (Luft et al. 2004, Methods [FT, PMC author manuscript]). The pilot of 14 people a median 30 months after stroke had no control group and reported gains on Fugl-Meyer, Wolf time and arm use that largely held at 8 weeks (Whitall et al. 2000 [ABS]). In the randomised substudy of 21, functional outcomes did not differ between BATRAC and dose-matched exercise; the within-group Fugl-Meyer gain of 12 percent in BATRAC was not different from the control group's (Luft et al. 2004, Results and Discussion [FT]). In the 111-person RCT the gains were comparable (Fugl-Meyer change 1.1 against 1.9 points for dose-matched exercise), BATRAC was not superior, and neither change reached the 10 percent of the scale the authors used as clinically significant (Whitall et al. 2011, Abstract and Discussion [FT, PMC author manuscript]). Ten percent of the 66-point upper-limb Fugl-Meyer is 6.6 points (arithmetic).
- Meta-analyses that disagree. Cauraugh et al. (2010) pooled 25 comparisons from 366 patients: SMD 0.734 (95 percent CI 0.489 to 0.979), I2 63 percent, with the largest effects for BATRAC (0.842) and coupled bilateral movement with EMG-triggered stimulation of the wrist and finger extensors (1.142). Controls ranged from unilateral training to placebo stimulation, one outcome was taken per study with kinematic or EMG measures used when no clinical scale was reported, 8 of 25 comparisons did not describe randomisation, and the authors' own funnel plot showed possible bias (Methods, Results [FT, PMC author manuscript]). A published response from the Cochrane authors followed (Pollock et al. 2011 [META]). van Delden et al. (2012) point out that the two positive meta-analyses pooled outcomes from all levels of the ICF with kinematics and compared bilateral training with a mixture of controls or none; their own nine RCTs (452 patients) found the two approaches similarly effective, with a marginal effect favouring unilateral training in chronic mild paresis (SMD 0.34, 0.04 to 0.63) (Introduction, Results [FT]).
- The Cochrane review: 18 studies (549 participants). Against usual care, ADL SMD 0.25 (-0.14 to 0.63), arm function -0.07, hand function -0.04; against other arm interventions, ADL -0.25 (-0.57 to 0.08), arm -0.20, hand -0.21; four of the included trials trained wrist and finger extension (Coupar et al. 2010, Abstract and included studies [FT]). The Cochrane overview found moderate-quality evidence that unilateral arm training may be more effective than bilateral (6 trials, 375 people) and concluded the evidence does not support bilateral training as a replacement for unilateral training (Pollock et al. 2014, Abstract and summary table [FT, PMC page]). Two other syntheses agree: no significant effect of bilateral arm training on any outcome in 22 RCTs, 823 people (Veerbeek et al. 2014, section 5 [FT]); "similar or inferior" in 23 RCTs, 1,104 people, and not recommended (Hatem et al. 2016, Bilateral training [FT]). One later meta-analysis found a 2.21-point Fugl-Meyer advantage (0.12 to 4.30, I2 86 percent) but no difference in function (SMD 0.25, -0.02 to 0.52) (Chen et al. 2019, Abstract [FT]); 2.21 points is a third of 6.6 (arithmetic).
- The closest trials to finger training: modified BATRAC aimed at wrist and finger extensors was not superior to dose-matched conventional therapy in 60 patients 1 to 6 months after stroke (van Delden et al. 2013 [ABS]), and the coupling between the hands did not change more after bilateral than after unilateral training (van Delden et al. 2015 [ABS]).
- Priming. Active-passive bilateral therapy (the unaffected wrist drives the affected one through a device, as described in Cauraugh et al. 2010 [FT]) for 10 to 15 minutes before practice gave better arm function a month later in 32 chronic patients (Stinear et al. 2008 [ABS]); device-assisted mirror-symmetric movement before physiotherapy made 57 subacute patients three times as likely to reach their recovery plateau by 12 weeks (Stinear et al. 2014 [ABS]). Both are passive or device-assisted priming, not an active press task.
- Mirror therapy. The 1996 paper studied ten arm amputees in a mirror box. Patient D.S., making mirror-symmetric movements with his eyes shut, felt the phantom stay frozen; when he looked in the mirror he felt it move. The authors considered that motor commands and feedback from the intact arm might reach the other hemisphere through the corpus callosum and concluded this information was not sufficient on its own; stroke appears as a possible implication in the last paragraph (Ramachandran and Rogers-Ramachandran 1996, Summary, mirror-box method, section (e), conclusions [FT]). The first stroke study was a crossover in which bilateral movements were made with a mirror or with transparent plastic between the limbs (Altschuler et al. 1999 [META]; design as tabulated in Thieme et al. 2018 [FT]). Thieme et al. (2018) pooled 62 studies (1,982 participants): motor function SMD 0.47 (0.27 to 0.67), with subacute (0.45) and chronic (0.43, 0.06 to 0.81) subgroups. When the control group made the same movements with an unrestricted view of both limbs, the effect was not significant (SMD 0.27, -0.05 to 0.59; 14 studies, 474 people) (Abstract, Comparisons 3 and 4 [FT, PMC page]). Seven RCTs gave non-significant effects in another synthesis (Veerbeek et al. 2014, section 9 [FT]), while Hatem et al. (2016) rated mirror therapy superior to bimanual and symmetric training [FT]. The sources conflict on the size of the mirror effect; none of them attributes that effect to bilateral movement made without the mirror.
- Who might benefit. McCombe Waller and Whitall (2008) argue bilateral retraining is needed for bilateral tasks, that benefit depends on severity, and that the non-paretic arm is commonly slowed in bilateral movement ([FT, PMC author manuscript], Sections 1 and 2).

Bearing. The thesis may say that Mirror delivers simultaneous bilateral practice of homologous fingers and measures the timing between the hands. It may not call the mode mirror therapy, borrow mirror-therapy effect sizes (Mirror is the "unrestricted view" control those trials beat by an amount that was not significant), cite Ramachandran 1996 for interlimb coupling, or describe bilateral training as effective: the best syntheses find it no better than usual care or one-arm training.

### Q2. What does healthy bimanual timing look like, which hand leads, and what do cues and feedback do?

- Stability. When hands oscillate faster, the anti-phase pattern switches to the in-phase pattern with simultaneous activation of homologous muscles (Kelso 1984 [ABS]; modelled by Haken, Kelso and Bunz 1985 [META]). This is continuous movement.
- Discrete movements. After an auditory go signal, two-handed reaches to targets of very different difficulty start and end together, with peak velocity and acceleration almost synchronous (Kelso, Southard and Goodman 1979, both papers [ABS]). The coupling replicates with ordinary feedback but integrated Lissajous feedback lets people decouple the hands (Shea, Boyle and Kovacs 2012 [ABS]). In 19 adults aged 18 to 23, mean signed lags at movement onset and peak speed stayed within 12 ms of zero in every condition and hand side was never a significant factor; at movement end the absolute lag was 67 ms (same-sized targets) and 106 ms (different sizes), with the attended hand arriving first (Sardar et al. 2023, Results [FT, PMC page]). Removing visual feedback increased synchrony (Miller and Smyth 2012 [ABS]). For discrete simultaneous EMG reaction times, synchronisation errors were smaller for homologous than non-homologous pairings (Nakamura, Sajiki and Taniguchi 1977 [ABS]); bimanual responses to a single go signal are grouped on nearly every trial (Miller and Ulrich 2008 [ABS]).
- Finger-level gaps. Ten healthy adults (26 to 47 years) making paced bimanual finger opposition with eyes closed, timed by a glove at 1 kHz, had a mean absolute inter-hand onset interval of 24.0, 21.8 and 18.5 ms at 1, 1.5 and 2 Hz (SE 4.5, 2.5 and 2.3, so between-person SD about 7 to 14 ms, arithmetic), with no difference between dominant and non-dominant hands in the single-hand measures; people with MS were near double (Bonzano et al. 2008, Methods and Results [FT, PMC page]). In 80 healthy adults the same interval was 18.87 ms (SE 0.87) against 41.92 ms in MS, and the log interval had a between-person SD of 0.45 (Bonzano et al. 2013, Results and Table 1 [FT]); the 10th to 90th percentile spread that implies is a factor of about 3.2 (arithmetic).
- Signed asynchrony in tapping. In 27 right-handed students tapping both index fingers every 400 ms, the group mean between-hand phase difference was -0.7 ms (SD 4.95 ms across people), a minuscule left lead; the largest individual mean was 12.1 ms and the median absolute individual mean 3.9 ms; the authors saw no a priori reason for one hand to lead (Helmuth and Ivry 1996, Experiment 1 [FT]).
- Which hand leads. Continuous ellipse tracing and forearm rotation: the dominant hand leads by about 25 ms at any speed (Stucchi and Viviani 1993; Viviani et al. 1998 [ABS]); rhythmic bimanual Fitts tapping: preferred-hand lead (Amazeen, Ringenbach and Amazeen 2005 [ABS]); circle drawing: lead depends on direction and mode, and dominance does not generally decide it (Franz, Rowse and Ballantine 2002 [ABS]). Discrete bimanual reaction time: 40 right-handers and 40 left-handers to a central visual stimulus, dominant-hand RT faster on average, more dominant-led trials in right-handers than left-handers with similar absolute differences, and the conclusion that dominance does not generally determine the lead (Shen and Franz 2005 [ABS]); index-finger force pulses to a finger stimulus gave significant right-left differences within people, in both directions across people (Kaluzny, Palmeri and Wiesendanger 1994 [ABS]); a bimanual RT cost for the right index finger in 12 right-handers (Taniguchi et al. 2001 [ABS]). In unimanual RT the two hands of 32 right-handers did not differ, and the literature the authors review splits three ways, with equal latencies most common (Nisiyama and Ribeiro-do-Valle 2014, Introduction and Results [FT]).
- Bimanual cost. With same-sized targets, bimanual reaches started 29 to 46 ms later than unimanual ones (445 and 419 ms unimanual against 491 and 448 ms bimanual) (Sardar et al. 2023, Table 1 [FT]); in a simple task bimanual RTs were shorter than unimanual and in a choice task longer (Nisiyama and Ribeiro-do-Valle 2014 [FT]).
- Cues, instructions and feedback. Adding auditory feedback to each tap reduced timing variability and removing it from one hand reduced the bimanual advantage (Drewing and Aschersleben 2003 [ABS]); asynchrony between the thumbs of 36 right-handers was larger when told to react "as strongly as possible" than "as quickly as possible", and smaller in women (Gutnik, Hudson and Nicholson 2009 [ABS]); gaze leads the hand it is on (Sardar et al. 2023 [FT]); force rise times and amplitudes of two index-finger pulses are correlated, with temporal control dominating (Rinkenauer, Ulrich and Wing 2001 [ABS]).

Bearing. M1's direction is well supported, but its 60 ms bound is about three times the healthy absolute gap measured with a 1 kHz system, so it cannot fail for a working rig. Kelso et al. 1979 and Bonzano et al. 2008 and 2013 are the right anchors, not Kelso 1984. For M2 the literature gives no stable direction for discrete presses: a 25 ms dominant lead appears in continuous rotation only, tapping shows -0.7 ms, and discrete RT shows individual leads both ways. A group lead, if any, should be expected under about 10 ms.

### Q3. What happens to between-hand timing after stroke, and what could a gap measure show in patients?

- Coupling persists but bends toward the slower limb. In 16 chronic patients reaching with both arms at once, the two arms did not differ in movement time or peak velocity, and the paretic arm was fastest when moving with the other (McCombe Waller et al. 2006 [ABS]). In 30 chronic patients the non-paretic arm lengthened its movement (10 percent lower peak velocity) so both hands hit the targets together in 81 percent of trials, while the paretic arm sped up 4 percent (Rose and Winstein 2005 [ABS]). Bilateral elbow flexion lengthened RT and movement time in both arms of 25 patients (Dickstein et al. 1993 [ABS]). The expected direction of coupling is from the slower, paretic limb to the faster one, and entrainment of the paretic limb has rarely been verified (Sleimen-Malkoun et al. 2011, sections on discrete tasks and coupling [FT]).
- Coupling strength. Weaker after stroke, with spatial coupling affected more than temporal, detectable in mild hemiparesis (Rose and Winstein 2013 [ABS]); mildly impaired synchrony in reasonably strong patients (Kilbreath et al. 2006 [ABS]); dominance effects altered by the side of the lesion (Lewis and Byblow 2004 [ABS]); patients locked into in-phase 1:1 patterns (Rice and Newell 2004 [ABS]); training did not change coupling differently (van Delden et al. 2015 [ABS]); bimanual coordination is under-measured in rehabilitation (Kantak, Jax and Wittenberg 2017 [ABS]).
- Force. In bimanual wrist and finger extension the less-impaired hand's force lags the impaired hand's and the two are less coordinated (Lodha, Coombes and Cauraugh 2012 [ABS]); bimanual force variability is higher after stroke and asymmetric, the paretic hand more variable in 78 to 89 percent of patients (Kang and Cauraugh 2014, Results [FT]).
- Mirror movements. During paretic-hand squeezing, involuntary movements of the unaffected hand were found in 70 to 78 percent of 23 patients (Nelles et al. 1998 [ABS]).
- A gap measure in another disease. The absolute inter-hand interval doubled in MS, discriminated patients with very low disability from healthy adults, correlated with disability (r 0.56) and with callosal integrity (Bonzano et al. 2008, 2013 [FT]).
- What the shipped rules do with asymmetric hands (simulation, illustration only, Section 7): a left hand 60 ms later with a 60 ms gap SD gives a median gap of 62 ms and no gated trials; 200 ms later with 150 ms SD gates 16 percent of trials and drives the pace to the 10 BPM floor at some point; 400 ms later with 250 ms SD gates 56 percent, the hit rate falls to 0.41, the pace stays between 10 and 24 BPM and 19 percent of trials are cued in recovery.

Bearing. In a patient study the gap could separate patients from healthy adults and track severity cross-sectionally, but a smaller gap can mean the faster hand waited, so it has to be read beside each hand's RT against its own one-hand RT. Change with training is unproven. The fixed 350 ms gate with a controller that can only slow the pace cannot make the task easier on the dimension a hemiparetic pair fails.

### Q5. The synchrony gate, what counts as a clean pair, and the scoring

- Gate: fixed at 350 ms whatever the pace (mirror.py 140-149, 423-442). Healthy hands: 0.4 percent of trials gated, all from simulated ungrouped responses (simulation). Asymmetric hands: 16 and 56 percent gated in the moderate and marked illustrations (Q3).
- Clean pair: both hands' presses logged and no wrong finger (engine 9259-9274; notebook 22113). Gated pairs stay in the gap, so the gate does not censor it (engine 6021-6033). One-sided trials, where the window closed before the second hand pressed, are excluded; they are the widest pairs, so the window censors the gap (Q6). The second hand's press is still in raw.csv as a `press` event (engine 557-577).
- Error types: a one-sided Miss is logged as `timeout` although one hand pressed (engine row construction), against the comment in mirror.py 478-482 that only a no-press Miss should be a timeout.
- Score: the tier comes from the later press, so a 5 ms pair and a 300 ms pair with the same later press earn the same points; synchrony is pass or fail at 350 ms (code). A press that misses the window is charged as an idle press when it arrives: 5.8 idle-press charges per simulated healthy 32-trial block (simulation).
- Perfect cap: a sub-100 ms later press is paid as Great because the rest is a deterministic function of BPM and the notebook drops presses under 100 ms (mirror.py 404-422; notebook `ANTICIPATION_MS` 1625). Consistent with Reaction and Adaptive, but a single hand's press under 100 ms still counts as that hand's press and as a full-quality hit.

Bearing. The gate is well placed for healthy adults and nearly inert. The scoring rewards the speed of the slower hand, not togetherness, which is acceptable for a measurement block but means the game never tells a player how close the pair was. One-sided trials hide the widest pairs and can be recovered from raw.csv.

### Q6. The adaptive cadence underneath, late presses, sub-100 ms presses and per-hand weighting

- The pace moves the window from 2.25 s to about 0.5 s in a healthy block (simulation, peak median 108 BPM). As the window tightens, pairs whose second press is late become one-sided misses: 1.2 per block under the shipped climb against none at a frozen 24 BPM. The mean absolute gap is 25.0 ms under the climb against 30.5 ms frozen (median gap 19.1 against 20.5 ms; 10 percent trimmed mean 20.9 against 22.5 ms) (simulation). The engine's mean exceeds the block median by more than 10 ms in 16 percent of shipped blocks and 38 percent of frozen ones (simulation).
- Late presses: a press on the previous trial's finger within 150 ms of a new cue occurred on 0.06 percent of simulated trials, because the rest after each trial is at least min(60/BPM, 1 s) (simulation, code). Mirror does not need Adaptive's `carry_ms`; its late presses become idle-press charges instead (Q5).
- Sub-100 ms presses: none in the simulation (no anticipation was modelled); with the finger unpredictable among three, a correct guess pays one time in three (arithmetic). Sharing Reaction's and Adaptive's rule keeps the modes consistent.
- Device drop: a press-less close while a board is down is voided in the row by the engine (engine 9179-9197), but Mirror has already fed it to the controller as a miss (mirror.py 448-453), so a one-board drop drives the pace down and into recovery. Adaptive guards against this; Mirror does not.
- Per-hand weighting: the weakness bias is per finger pair, from pair hits; a pair failing because one hand is weak is cued more, but nothing targets a hand; in recovery 70 percent of picks go to the best pair (code).

Bearing. "Report bpm_min and bpm_max" is the right instinct, but in Mirror the pace is a deadline, and the window it sets censors the gap in the opposite direction to the claim limit's wording. Report the window in ms and the count of one-sided trials beside every gap. Add the device-drop guard; the late-press credit is not needed.

### Q8. Reliability, the right summaries, and how many trials

### Q10. Instructions, feedback, rewards and safety for a hemiparetic hand

- Instructions set how people trade force, speed and synchrony: telling people to react strongly widened the asynchrony between the thumbs compared with telling them to react quickly (Gutnik, Hudson and Nicholson 2009 [ABS]); verbal instructions and payoffs set the speed-accuracy balance (as reviewed in `docs/research/deep/adaptive.md` Q11). Mirror has no GET READY line, so the only instruction is the PRESS TOGETHER label, while the score pays speed.
- Feedback: a gated pair shows the generic "miss" wording, which names neither hand nor the direction; the feedback bank's own rules ask for the direction ("behind", "ahead of") and the next action (`feedback_bank.py` docstring R2, R3). The trained quantity, the gap, appears only on the results screen.
- Attention and vision move the lead (Sardar et al. 2023 [FT]; Miller and Smyth 2012 [ABS]); the two lit tiles are close together for the index finger and far apart for the little finger (code), so visual demands change with the finger.
- Rewards: the pace multiplier and streak bonus reward speed and runs of hits; for a hemiparetic pair, the gate failure loop (Q3) means most trials end in "next one" wording while the pace sits at the floor.
- Hemiparetic hand: the non-paretic hand tends to wait (Rose and Winstein 2005 [ABS]; Dickstein et al. 1993 [ABS]); involuntary movements of the unaffected hand are common (Nelles et al. 1998 [ABS]); a bilateral force deficit lowers force in two-hand tasks even in healthy hands (Li et al. 2001 [ABS]); a finger that cannot relax below its release trigger stays latched and its later presses are not detected (calibration_profile.py 65-80); recovery mode puts 70 percent of cues on the best pair. These belong in the session procedure (screening, rest, stopping rules, consent wording), not in an approvals task.

Bearing. Add a short instruction that matches the scoring, a feedback line that names the hand that came in behind, and, for any patient use, a synchrony requirement that adapts to the person rather than a pace that collapses.

### 6.2 Reliability and change expectations

- One 32-trial block: ICC about 0.74 for the median absolute gap and 0.82 for a 10 percent trimmed mean, 0.59 for the engine's mean, 0.59 for the signed median (simulation); 0.75 for the absolute interval one month apart in the literature (Bonzano et al. 2013 [FT]).
- Block-to-block change in a person's median gap with nothing changing: SD about 6 ms, so a change under about 12 ms is within noise (simulation, arithmetic).
- Within one block: no narrowing is expected; a 5 ms narrowing would be detected in 28 percent of cohorts of 10 (simulation).

### 6.3 Claims to avoid

- That Mirror is mirror therapy, or any mirror-therapy effect size applied to it.
- That bilateral training works, or that the mode trains recovery; the best syntheses find no advantage (Q1).
- That Ramachandran 1996 shows interlimb coupling or the "unaffected hand drags the affected one" idea.
- That M1 shows anything about the hands; it checks the rig.
- Any dominant-hand lead from M2 without a bench-measured inter-board offset and swapped boards; and any directional prediction for it.
- That two boards stamp presses independently, or a fixed "7 ms" resolution; give the measured bench figure.
- That a narrower gap within a block, or at a faster pace, is better coordination (censoring, Q6).
- That a smaller gap in a patient means a better paretic hand (the other hand may wait, Q3).
- Per-finger synchrony differences (as few as about 5 pairs per finger in a 32-trial block, arithmetic; pad and screen confounds).
- RT or force asymmetry from a Mirror block (None by construction until logged).

### Simulation assumptions (Sections 1 to 6)

SIM A, timing (`sim_merger.py`). Verbatim copies of `hardware/multi_serial.py` (`_emit_paired_if_ready`, `_push_combined`), `hardware/fsr_detector.py` (`FSRDetector`, default `Calibration`: trigger 320 counts, value EMA 0.35, debounce 100 ms) and `hardware/source.py`, with `time.perf_counter` patched to a simulated clock; the merge-loop body replicated (one sample per board per pass, 0.02 ms a pass while samples flow, a 2.2 ms sleep when none arrived). Boards sample every 5.025 ms, the left board 60 ppm fast, phases random. Smooth delivery: arrival 2 ms after acquisition plus an exponential 0.5 ms; packet delivery: packets every 20.1 ms with a random phase per board, arriving 1.2 ms after the boundary, samples 0.02 ms apart. Force: baseline 265 counts, noise SD 1 count, linear rise of 80 to 200 counts over 40 to 120 ms, 350 ms hold, 80 ms release. True event: the noise-free ramp crossing the trigger; true gap normal with SD 25 ms; 600 pairs per case, and twelve 30-pair sessions per delivery type for the session bias.

SIM B and C, Mirror blocks (`sim_mirror.py`, `sim_mirror_cohort.py`, `sim_mirror_rel.py`, `sim_mirror_extra.py`, `sim_mirror_patient.py`, `sim_mirror_carry.py`). Verbatim copies of `game/modes/mirror.py`, `analytics/adaptive.py`, `game/scheduling.py` and `game/scoring.py`, driven at 60 Hz frames by a fake clock and a spy engine that applies the engine's three-miss recovery rule; shipped settings (start 24 BPM, band 0.65 to 0.80, step 10, bounds 10 to 180 BPM, weakness bias 2.5, floor 0.15, 4 fingers times 8, gate 350 ms, window 0.90 of 60/BPM, rest min(60/BPM, 1 s)). Person: decision time ex-Gaussian with mu normal (400, 45) ms plus a 30 ms bimanual cost and finger offsets of 0, 5, 20 and 15 ms, sigma normal (35, 8), tau normal (60, 15); up to 30 ms faster when the mean press nears the window; wrong-finger probability 0.02 plus 0.65 times exp(-(RT - 150)/45), with both hands pressing the same wrong finger and correcting 250 ms later; each hand equals the common time plus or minus half the person's signed bias plus independent noise, so the within-person SD of the signed gap is 25 ms (multiplied per person by exp(normal(0, 0.40)) in the reliability, rig-variant and W2-power runs, matching Bonzano et al. 2013's spread); person signed bias normal (minus the dominant lead, 8 ms); 2 percent of pairs ungrouped, one hand delayed uniform 150 to 700 ms; device error added to each hand from SIM A (gap SD 2.7 ms smooth, 8.4 ms in packets) and, where stated, a fixed right-board offset of plus or minus 9 ms. Cohorts of 8 to 20 drawn from 200 to 500 simulated people; M1 as `_mean_check` (t interval upper end below 60 ms); M2 as `_paired_check` (one-sided Wilcoxon, p below 0.05, no Holm adjustment); W2 as `_within_block_row` (paired t interval of last-third minus first-third medians entirely below 0); ICC(2,1) between two independent blocks per person with no change; the asymmetric-hand runs set the left hand 60, 200 or 400 ms later with gap SDs of 60, 150 and 250 ms and slower decision times (500, 600 and 700 ms). The model gives a healthy median absolute gap of about 18 to 20 ms, close to Bonzano et al.'s 18.9 ms, and hit rates and paces like the Adaptive pilot; it has not been checked against any two-board recording, because none exists.
