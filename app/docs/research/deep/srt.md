### 1.2 The material

- The lab sequence is squares 1-3-2-1-4-3-2-4-1-3 (`srt_setup.py` 57; the script's `v n b v m n b m v n`, line 231). Structure (arithmetic, cyclic, since the sequence repeats without a break): counts 3, 2, 3 and 2 (squares 1 and 3 are 30 percent of trials); no square follows itself, the cycle join included; every square has two successors (1 goes to 3 twice and 4 once, 3 to 2 twice and 1 once, 2 to 1 or 4, 4 to 3 or 1); the previous two squares fix the next one at 6 of 10 positions (2, 5, 6, 7, 9 and 10) and the previous three fix all ten; two reversals per cycle (1-3-1 and 3-1-3, both at the cycle join); two runs of three neighbours (3-2-1 and 4-3-2); zero-order entropy 1.971 bits; first-order conditional entropy 0.951 bits against 1.585 bits for random material without repeats; 8 of 10 transitions change hands in the two-hand layout; 4 of 10 go to a neighbouring square.
- Nissen and Bullemer's sequence 4-2-3-1-3-2-4-3-2-1 (via Schwarb and Schumacher 2012, "The Serial Reaction Time Task" [FT]) has counts 2, 3, 3 and 2, one reversal, previous-two prediction at 8 of 10 positions and a first-order conditional entropy of 1.151 bits (arithmetic). The lab sequence is not Nissen and Bullemer's under any relabelling of the squares, rotation or reversal (arithmetic, 24 relabellings by 10 rotations by 2 directions), and it is not the sequence of the lab's 2026 study either (1-3-1-4-3-2-4-2-3-1, which repeats square 1 at its cycle join; Leow et al. 2026, Fig. 1 [FT]).
- Random blocks use the script's own generator, step for step: a pool with equal counts, shuffled, then a random pick from the items that differ from the last (script 233-252; `random_targets` 184-208). Over 20,000 simulated 48-trial blocks the reversal share is 0.324 (10th to 90th percentile 0.239 to 0.413), the share of runs of three neighbours 0.113, hand changes 0.667, neighbour transitions 0.500, and each square is followed by each other square a third of the time (simulation). A learning block has reversal share 0.184, runs 0.204, hand changes 0.80 and neighbour transitions 0.40 over its trials 3 to 100 (arithmetic).
- Timing groups (script 12-16, 202, 254-266; `cyclical_pattern` 146-153, `random_chunk` 156-166): constant 500 ms; cyclical, the interval before positions 1 to 10 is 500, 250, 500, 750, 250, 500, 750, 250, 500 and 750 ms, so the long gaps come before positions 4, 7 and 10 (arithmetic); random, each run of ten intervals is a shuffle of three 250, four 500 and three 750 ms. All average 500 ms. In a cyclical learning block, the 39 trials that follow a 500 ms interval (trial 1 out) are positions 1, 3, 6 and 9, which are squares 1, 2, 3 and 1, never square 4 (simulation, 19, 10 and 10 trials). The random group has 39 or 40 such trials per block, on any square (arithmetic: four 500 ms intervals in each run of ten, 99 intervals per block).
- All random material for a session (practice, intervals, post-test) is drawn at the block start from a seeded generator and the seed is logged (`_build_steps` 245-278; engine 4541-4547, 4582-4590). The script drew it unseeded as it ran.
- The four tone files are byte-identical to the lab's (`assets/srt`, `archive/Webler EEG past program/sounds`; MD5 match, arithmetic). Fundamentals about 661, 700, 790 to 800 and 889 to 898 Hz; files 197 to 203 ms long, with the envelope below 10 percent of its peak after 114 to 132 ms; RMS levels -28.5, -28.5, -38.8 and -40.5 dBFS, so the tones for squares 3 and 4 (N and M) are about 10 to 12 dB quieter than those for squares 1 and 2, and their second harmonic is as strong as their fundamental (arithmetic, FFT of the files). The README's "about 200 ms" is the file length.

### 1.3 What the participant is told and shown

- Before the task the setup screen may be in view (its docstring says the participant often sits in front of it). For the cyclical group it reads "Gaps of 250, 500 and 750 ms, in a set order that follows the sequence", and for every group "About 16 min: practice, 8 learning blocks, a final test, then recall" (`srt_setup_screen.py` 55-64, 211-218). The musical experience prompt reads "ask: none, or years of lessons", so the question is put before the task (205-208).
- The messages are the script's, with "finger cards" and "lit card" for "grey squares" and "red" (`srt.py` 869-906). The instruction asks for speed only ("as quickly as you can"), as the script does (script 603-617). Nothing names a sequence until the recall screen asks "Did you notice a repeating sequence during the main task?" and asks for "the 10-item sequence you learned" (908-919; script 469-475). The yes or no answer to that question is never recorded, in the script or here.
- During blocks: the instruction line at the top, four finger cards on the light page, idle cards a pale grey, and the target card filled with its finger's colour (index orange, middle blue, ring near-black, little yellow) with a thicker border for 100 ms (`srt_screen.py` 1-21, 132-161; `theme.py` 39-46). The lit fills have relative luminances of 0.245, 0.329, 0.009 and 0.307 against about 0.77 for an idle card on a 0.95 page (arithmetic), so the step at the flash differs by location and is largest for the ring card. In the script, grey squares (#808080, relative luminance 0.216) turn red (#FF0000, 0.213) on black: a near-isoluminant colour change that is the same at every location (arithmetic from the PsychoPy colour names, script 143-145).
- Geometry: the script's squares are 0.13 normalised units wide with centres at 0.075 and 0.225 of the half-width either side of the middle, and its docstring says they were set close together to limit eye movements during EEG (script 18-19, 139-155). The app's cards are about 266 of 1,280 logical pixels wide and up to 440 of 800 tall, with centres at 142 and 427 pixels from the middle, 0.22 and 0.67 of the half-width (arithmetic from `srt_screen.py` 41-47, 112-130). On a 52 cm wide screen at 60 cm, the outer targets sit about 5.5 degrees from the centre in the script and about 16 degrees in the app (arithmetic).
- Practice feedback words use the page's colours ("Miss!" in the warning colour, since the script's white would vanish on the light page); no score, timer or halo; a Controls note in the corner on a keyboard (`srt_screen.py` 48-51, 209-223, 284-296).
- After the block the results screen shows LEARNING EFFECT (signed ms), ACCURACY and RECALL n of 10 (`screens.py` 7010, 7271-7305).
- There is no GET READY card and no byte 20 for an SRT block (engine docstring 4516-4534; `_begin_block` 5747). The lab package's README tells the researcher to pick the timing group and press START, and gives no spoken instruction for the participant (`EEG_Lab/README.md`, step 4).

### 1.4 Logging and exported files

- The script's three CSVs, under its names and in its column order, written into the block folder (`export_files` 942-986): `SRT_<who>_<group>_<stamp>.csv` with the script's 15 columns (`PERF_COLUMNS` 127-130) followed by hand, setup, learning_isi_ms, sequence, input, response_finger, onset_s, rsi_ms and flag (`PERF_EXTRA` 135-136); `SRT_SEQUENCE_<who>_<stamp>.csv`; and `SRT_RECALL_<who>_<group>_<stamp>.csv`, only once a recall is submitted.
- `rsi_ms` is the flip that showed the flash minus the flip that ended the previous trial (765-766): the interval plus one frame, plus any miss pause or feedback word. It is not the time from the press to the flash.
- Column contents that differ from the script: gender comes from the login's sex field; musical_experience is blank unless picked, where the script's dialog lists "0 - no experience" first (script 59-72); monitor_hz is the measured refresh rate (117 to 122 in the pilot), where the script writes the nominal 60 or 120 (script 179-190, 461).
- trials.csv rows go through `engine.log_srt_trial` (9615-9645): early_late is Hit for correct, Early for anticipatory_correct and Miss otherwise; the script's label rides in `feedback`; `stimulus` packs phase, block, position, square, interval and group (`srt.py` 771-803).
- raw.csv holds a stim event at each flash flip (557-563), keyboard presses (382-385), pad presses (engine 574-576) and the `srt_config` line with the seed (engine 4582-4590).
- metadata.json `block_summary.srt` is `block_stats` (1048-1147): per phase and learning block, n, accuracy, error rate, median correct RT (each block's first trial out, nothing over 1000 ms, flagged rows out), misses and anticipations; then `sequence_effect_ms` (post-test median minus block 8, 1063-1064), `sequence_effect_matched_ms` (against block 8 trials after a 500 ms interval, 1069-1072), `sequence_effect_prop` (divided by the post-test median, 1129-1130), `learning_speedup_ms` (block 1 minus block 8, 1073-1074), `accuracy_cost` (post-test error rate minus block 8's, 1075-1077) and the recall scores.

### 1.5 EEG markers

- Byte 30 is armed with the flash (`_arm_flash` 601-617) and written by the frame loop straight after the flip, with the flip time as `t_event` (engine 1061-1072; `_flush_eeg_stim` 1373-1387). Nothing else is sent per trial unless `srt.response_markers` is on (default off, `default.yaml` 929-930): then 100 plus lane for a correct press, 110 plus lane for a wrong one, 120 plus lane for an anticipation and 130 for a miss, sent at the trial's end with the press time as `t_event` (`_response_marker` 828-838; `eeg_trigger.py` 157-160).
- Block bytes 213 and 233 (219 if abandoned), no 20; 240 and 241 around the login session; 242 and 243 around a pause (`eeg_trigger.py` 168-176, 203; engine 5742-5751).
- The writer sends `bytes([code])`, then writes 0 on the first frame tick at least `pulse_ms` (8 ms in the lab build) later, which makes a pulse of 8 to 17 ms at 60 Hz, and keeps 12 ms of quiet before the next code (`eeg_lab.yaml` 33-39; `eeg_trigger.py` 875-922, 993-1027). For a 30 written right after a flip, the 0 comes after the next flip, about 16.7 ms later at 60 Hz (arithmetic).
- The script wrote 30 after the flip (`bytes(chr(30), 'UTF-8')`, one byte) and its 0 during the next loop iteration, before that iteration's flip, so its pulse lasted about as long as one scene draw at 60 Hz and about one frame at 120 Hz (script 204-207, 352-354, 383-386). It sent no block, session or response bytes.

## 2. Research questions and findings

### Q1. Is the platform's SRT the lab's script, and how far are both from Nissen and Bullemer's Experiment 1?

- Constants match the script: 100 ms flash, 2,500 ms deadline after it, 100 ms anticipation window, 200 ms miss pause, 200 ms feedback, 500 ms random-block interval, 1,000 ms block start, the cyclical list and the 3, 4, 3 random chunk (script 195-202, 254-266; `default.yaml` 904-915). The test `TheScriptsConstantsAreTheDefaults` reads the script's own constants (`tests/test_srt_mode.py` 171-217). The random generator, the accuracy labels, the recall rules and the three files' names and columns are the script's (code).
- On a simulated 60 Hz flip clock the replication shows six flash frames per trial, 516.7 ms from the frame that ended a trial to the next flash and one byte 30 on the flash flip; at 120 Hz, 12 frames and 508.3 ms (simulation; the 90 SRT tests pass at d48362f). The script gives the same counts: its interval loop draws the interval's frames after the frame that ended the trial and then flips the flash (script 322-350).
- Nissen and Bullemer's Experiment 1 (Schwarb and Schumacher 2012, "The Serial Reaction Time Task" [FT]): an asterisk at one of four positions, a compatible key under each, the asterisk gone at the response and the next trial 500 ms later; one group saw the sequence 4-2-3-1-3-2-4-3-2-1 ten times per 100-trial block, the other random order with no immediate repeats; eight blocks; Block by Group effects in RT and accuracy; 11 of 12 sequence participants noticed the sequence. The later standard measure is within each person: sequence blocks, an alternate or random block, then the sequence again (same source, "Measuring Sequence Learning" [FT]).

Deviations, intended or not, and whether they matter for the measures:

| Deviation from the script | Intended | Matters for |
|---|---|---|
| Display: light page, tall finger cards three times as far out, each location lit in a different colour and luminance (Section 1.3) | Yes (28 September) | EEG: eye movements and location-specific visual responses; comparison with the lab's data. Little for the RT contrast within a session |

### Q2. What timing does a participant actually get, and how good is the flip clock?

- The script counts frames and flips with PsychoPy, whose flips wait for the refresh. On Windows 10, PsychoPy's visual onset lagged a trigger sent with the flip by 7.10 ms (SD 0.35), and its RTs lagged by 12.05 ms (SD 0.35) with a 1 kHz button box; audio lagged 0.85 ms (SD 0.96) with the PTB backend pre-scheduled (Bridges et al. 2020, Table 2 and "PsychoPy implementation" [FT]). The lab's script used the sounddevice backend and set a latency mode that applies only to PTB, and called `play()` straight away (script 97-99, 345-346); PsychoPy's manual puts the other backends at a lag of 20 ms or more with several ms of variability, against about 5 ms for PTB in immediate mode (PsychoPy documentation, Sound playback [FT, documentation]).
- The replication flips with pygame-ce at vsync=1, which blocks until the refresh where the hardware and driver allow it and may be refused (pygame-ce documentation, display [FT, documentation]); if refused, the engine opens without vsync and logs a warning (engine 1674-1690), and the loop is capped by `clock.tick(120)` (1074). The flip time is `time.perf_counter()` after the flip returns, and the frame period is the median of the last 61 flip gaps (1065-1066, 1702-1720). The pygame-based Expyriment package lagged by 5.98 ms (SD 0.19) on visual onset and 106.83 ms (SD 13.72) on audio under Windows 10, and 29.02 ms visual and 33.83 ms RT under macOS (Bridges et al. 2020, Table 2 [FT]); the authors' advice is that only a photodiode and a microphone show these lags (Discussion [FT]). The study Mac's tone took 77 ms from dispatch to sound (`config/latency_profile.yaml`, measured 24 September 2026); the lab PC has no measured profile (`EEG_Lab/README.md` step 1).
- The true response-to-flash interval (arithmetic, 60 Hz, a press equally likely anywhere in a frame): in the script a key read just after flip k-1 ends the trial at flip k, the 30 interval frames follow and the flash is the 31st flip, so the interval from the key to the flash is 32 to 33 frames, about 530 to 550 ms (mean about 540); the replication reads a key at the start of the next frame loop and has the same 530 to 550 ms. With the pads, the press reaches the loop after the detector and the USB burst (7 to 11 ms and up to 20 ms; design doc Section 1.6; `EEG_Lab/README.md` Timing), so the interval from the force crossing to the flash averages about 560 ms (arithmetic under those delays). At 120 Hz the key interval is about 517 to 525 ms (arithmetic). The logged `rsi_ms` reads 516.7 ms at 60 Hz in every case (simulation).
- Practice adds the 200 ms feedback word to every interval, so a practice interval is about 717 ms from the end frame at 60 Hz (708 ms at 120 Hz) plus the time from the key to that frame (pilot at 120 Hz: median 716 ms from key handling to the flash).
- Without vsync on a 60 Hz panel the loop would run near 120 Hz and the logged monitor_hz would read about 120 (code), so monitor_hz doubles as a vsync check.
- The tone is dispatched as the flash is drawn, before the flip, with `tone_lead_ms` 0 (`srt.py` 583-587, 601-607; `default.yaml` 920-924). At the study Mac's 77 ms, the tone would sound about 60 to 77 ms after the flash (arithmetic); the script's own lag is unknown. The tone outlasts the flash in both programs (Section 1.2).

Bearing. Frame logic is the same in both programs. Absolute timing on the lab PC (flash to photons, tone to sound, and their jitter) is unknown for both until measured, and the "500 ms RSI" is nominal: about 530 to 550 ms with keys and about 560 ms with pads at 60 Hz. The thesis should say "500 ms nominal" and quote measured values once they exist.

### Q3. What is the structure of the lab sequence, and what can produce an RT effect without sequence learning?

- Type. Cohen, Ivry and Keele (1990, Experiments 3 and 4 [FT, repository]) call a sequence unique when each element has one successor, ambiguous when none has a unique successor, and hybrid when some do. Every square of the lab sequence has two successors once the cycle wraps (arithmetic), so at first order it is ambiguous, with two transitions biased two to one (1 to 3 and 3 to 2). Nissen and Bullemer's sequence has no unique successor either (arithmetic), although Schwarb and Schumacher call it hybrid [FT]. At second order the lab sequence has 6 unique contexts of 10 and Nissen and Bullemer's 8 (arithmetic). It is not an SOC sequence: SOC material balances location counts and first-order transitions (Reed and Johnson 1994, via Schwarb and Schumacher [FT]), and no 10-item SOC exists over four squares, since an SOC cycle uses each of the 12 ordered pairs once (arithmetic).
- Differences from the random blocks (arithmetic and simulation, Section 1.2): location counts 30, 20, 30 and 20 percent against 25; reversals 18 against 32 percent; runs 20 against 11 percent; hand changes in the two-hand layout 80 against 67 percent; neighbour transitions 40 against 50 percent.
- Evidence on artefacts. Shanks and St John (1994, Section 2.7 [FT, repository]) note that Nissen and Bullemer's counts (3, 3, 2, 2) let response biases speed sequence blocks, and report an experiment by Shanks and colleagues, run with Willingham et al.'s procedure, in which unaware learners sped up no more than a group given pseudorandom material with the same counts (Section 2.7.1; the primary report was not read). Random comparison material with more reversals gives an artefactual learning measure (Vaquero et al. 2006 [ABS]). Reversal trials were 37 and 39 ms slower than non-reversals on random trials, and 36 ms slower on sequence trials in the sham group, with V B N M and two hands (Jongkees et al. 2018, Table 1 [FT]). ASRT analyses drop trills because of pre-existing biases (Szegedi-Hallgató et al. 2019, section on trills [FT]). Studies avoid the problem by matching random material on counts and first-order transitions (Lum et al. 2023, Method [FT]; Lum et al. 2024, SRT task [FT]; Lum et al. 2025, SRT task [FT]), by excluding trills and runs (Stark-Inbar et al. 2017, SRT methods [FT, PMC page]), by matching reversals and hand switches (Jongkees et al. 2018, Method [FT]), or by matching counts and transitions (Shin and Ivry 2002, Method [FT, repository]).
- Sizes for a person who learns nothing (simulation, block 8 against the post-test with the lab's exclusions): a reversal cost of 37 ms gives a mean artefact of 4.9 ms and raises the one-sided false positive rate per person from 5 to 10 percent; with a little finger 40 ms slower as well, 6.9 ms and 13 percent; a 20 ms reversal cost gives 2.5 ms. Learning location counts alone gives 1.2 to 2.4 ms at 25 to 50 ms per bit (7.3 ms at an unrealistic 150 ms per bit) (arithmetic, Hyman-type model). Finger differences alone shift the plain effect by 0 to 2 ms (simulation).
- First-order learning is learning, not an artefact: a participant who learns only the first-order transitions faces 0.945 bits per trial in sequence blocks against 1.585 in random blocks, worth about 16 to 32 ms at 25 to 50 ms per bit (arithmetic). The plain effect therefore mixes first-order transition learning with knowledge of the whole order.

Bearing. For a group effect of tens of ms, the structural artefacts are a few ms; for one participant they double the false positive rate. A reversal-free and a location-reweighted effect should sit beside the plain one, and the thesis should call the result sequence learning, not knowledge of the full ten-item order. The reversal sentence in the thesis has the direction backwards.

### Q4. Which learning measures, and what sizes should healthy adults show?

- Exclusions. Errors and RTs over 1,000 ms leave the lab's RT analyses (Leow et al. 2025, Data analysis [FT, preprint]: errors 4.51 to 7.01 percent, slow trials 1.78 to 2.50 percent; Leow et al. 2026, Data cleaning [FT]: 6.10 and 2.18 percent). Dropping each block's first trial is this project's choice (Section 1.7). The lab now models single trials with a Student-t likelihood and maximal random effects (Leow et al. 2026, Data analysis [FT]); generalised mixed models with a Gamma or inverse Gaussian family fit raw RT without transformation (Lo and Andrews 2015, "The dependent variable" and "The link function" [FT]).
- The missing return to the sequence. The standard measure flanks the random block with sequence blocks (Schwarb and Schumacher [FT]); without it, fatigue or loss of vigilance adds to the effect. Lum et al. (2024, Results [FT]) ran the random block last and used practice against the final random block to show no fatigue (422 ms against 407 ms, d = 0.56). Here practice ran at about 717 ms intervals with a feedback word on every trial (Q2), so practice minus post-test mixes the interval change with general practice.
- The timing-matched effect matches only the interval's length. In the cyclical group it uses 39 trials from squares 1, 2 and 3 only (Section 1.2), so a slow little finger alone would add 10.7 ms (simulation). In the random group the matched trials sit in a variable-interval context, where RT depends on the current and the previous interval (Los et al. 2014, section on the asymmetric sequential effect [FT]), while the post-test is constant.
- The proportional effect divides by the post-test median, which removes overall speed from the comparison (code).
- Errors and anticipations. Sequence groups were more accurate (Nissen and Bullemer, via Schwarb and Schumacher [FT]). The script counts presses up to 100 ms before the flash; the lab's 2026 study instead counted responses faster than about 182 ms, a share that grew across blocks and was higher in musicians (Leow et al. 2026, Results [FT]). A longer RSI produces more premature responses and more verbal report (Haider and Frensch 2009 [ABS]).
- Published sizes, none from this exact design and none on force pads (a Europe PMC search on 1 October 2026 found no SRT with force-sensing keys): about 90 ms of slowing when random order replaced five- and six-item unique or hybrid sequences under tone counting (Cohen, Ivry and Keele 1990, Experiment 3 [FT, repository]); 52 ms (SE 5) with correlated timing and 14 ms (SE 7) without, 8-element ambiguous sequence, response board, two hands (Shin and Ivry 2002, Experiment 1 [FT, repository]); 36.1 ms (SD 23.6) late and 11.2 ms (SD 22.5) mid-run, 12-element sequence, V B N M right hand, about 200 ms from press to target (Stark-Inbar et al. 2017 [FT, PMC page]); 91 and 111 ms after 1,152 trials of a 12-item SOC (Destrebecqz et al. 2005, Results [FT, PMC page]); d = 1.09 after 300 trials of a 10-item first-order sequence on a response panel (Lum et al. 2023, Results [FT]); t(21) = 4.65 for random minus last sequence block, 12-item sequence (Beaulieu et al. 2014, Behavioral results [FT]); RT falling 39.7 ms per block in the lab's own explicit audiovisual task, with no random block (Leow et al. 2026, Results [FT]).

Bearing. Keep the plain effect as the lab's headline. Read beside it a reversal-free and reweighted effect, a per-person interval and the anticipation measures, and never quote the matched effect without its composition. A design with a 10-item sequence, a 500 ms interval and likely awareness should give effects at the top of or above the published range, but there is no published number to test against.

### Q5. How reliable is the learning score, and what can one participant or a handful show?

- Retest: pooled r = 0.28 (0.16 to 0.40), 0.30 (0.18 to 0.42) with every effect size and 0.37 (0.20 to 0.51) without the authors' own laboratory; no moderator significant; every pooled retest study used SOC material at 0 to 250 ms, and the two deterministic studies gave 0.23 (Oliveira et al. 2023, Results and Tables 1 and 2 [FT]). Split-half: 0.63 (0.52 to 0.72), or 0.66 with every effect size (same [FT]). Single studies: 0.07 deterministic, 0.38 deterministic, 0.47 probabilistic, and 0.70 in one adult study with 1,500 trials, the same sequence and 2 to 3 days apart (West et al. 2021 [META], values in Oliveira et al. 2024, Introduction [FT]).
- One participant (simulation, block 8 against the 48-trial post-test, lab exclusions, medians): from trial noise alone the estimate has an SD of 13 ms at a trial SD of about 64 ms and 18 ms at about 94 ms; a bootstrap 95 percent interval is about 52 to 74 ms wide; a true 40 ms effect clears a one-sided rank test 98 or 84 percent of the time, a true 20 ms effect 58 or 38 percent.
- A handful (simulation, one-sample t on per-person effects): with a true mean of 40 ms and a between-person SD of 25 ms, power is 0.48 at n = 3, 0.83 at n = 5 and 0.97 at n = 8; with an SD of 50 ms, 0.24, 0.43 and 0.65. At a true 20 ms, n = 8 gives 0.60 or 0.28.
- Group comparisons (arithmetic): d = 0.8 needs 26 per group; if the score's split-half reliability is 0.63, a true d of 0.8 is observed as about 0.63 and needs 40 per group; three groups at f = 0.25 need 158 people in all.

Bearing. The single EEG session gives one person's effect with a ±26 to 37 ms interval, enough to see a large effect and not a small one. A handful can show a group effect if it is near 40 ms or more. Differences between timing groups are out of reach, and an individual score is not a trait.

### Q6. What can the recall show about awareness, and does asking change later blocks or tasks?

- Chance (simulation, 100,000 random 10-item recalls of the lab sequence): positional score mean 2.50, 95th percentile 5, P(above 5) 0.02 with repeats allowed and 0.03 without; triplet share 0.278 (SD 0.192) without repeats, 95th percentile 0.625, P(above 0.63) 0.026, and 0.156 with repeats; longest run 3.46 (95th 5) without repeats and 2.93 (95th 4) with; best rotation about 5 of 10. A correct recall begun anywhere but position 1 scores 0 to 3 positionally (arithmetic).
- Partial knowledge (simulation): someone who follows the sequence's successor after a known pair half the time beats the triplet 95th percentile 31 percent of the time and the longest-run 95th percentile 27 percent of the time; at 80 percent, 69 and 56 percent; with full knowledge, 96 and 83 percent. The positional score stays near chance unless the recall starts at position 1.
- What recall can say. It is an inclusion-only generation test, which cannot separate implicit from explicit contributions; that needs an exclusion test as well (Destrebecqz and Cleeremans 2001 [ABS]; Schwarb and Schumacher, "Measures of explicit knowledge" [FT]). An awareness test must tap the knowledge that drives performance and be sensitive to all of it before a null result means anything (Shanks and St John 1994, Section 2.3.2, the Information and Sensitivity Criteria [FT, repository]). Willingham, Nissen and Bullemer (1989 [ABS]) found a subgroup who learned while generating the sequence at chance; Shanks and St John reread that result as frequency learning and conclude that most participants in sequence-learning studies hold fragments of the sequence consciously (Sections 2.7.1 and 2.7.6 [FT, repository]); typical awareness tests are underpowered, with a meta-analytic dz of 0.31 and power about 0.21 at the median n of 16 (Vadillo et al. 2016, Results [FT]). Verbal report is argued to be the sensitive and specific measure, and the choice of measure changes conclusions (Rünger and Frensch 2010 [ABS]). Robertson (2007 [FT, PMC page]) defines implicit learning behaviourally by poor free recall or recognition.
- Base rates. 11 of 12 noticed Nissen and Bullemer's sequence (via Schwarb and Schumacher [FT]); with mixed-deterministic material and the sequence concealed, 20 of 23 believed there was a sequence and chose the next item correctly 0.62 of the time against a chance of 0.2 (Barth et al. 2025, Post-experimental interview [FT]); the lab describes its own 500 ms audiovisual task as explicit sequence learning (Leow et al. 2026, Abstract [FT]); Stark-Inbar et al. treated a random-block slowing over 200 ms as a sign of explicit knowledge [FT, PMC page].
- Drivers present here: a 500 ms interval with a window that rewards pressing before the flash (Haider and Frensch 2009 [ABS]); a short deterministic sequence (explicit knowledge helps deterministic SRTs, Stefaniak et al. 2008 [ABS]); informative tones that make the sequence a melody; every block starting at position 1 after a 1 s pause (code); and setup screen wording that names a sequence and a recall (Section 1.3).
- Asking. The recall question comes after every SRT block, so it cannot touch the SRT's own measures (code). For later sequence tasks it tells the participant that sequences exist; with deterministic material, explicit knowledge lets people switch from stimulus-based to plan-based control (Barth et al. 2025, Abstract and Discussion [FT]). Leaving Muscle Memory out of the Lab session is consistent with this.
- The answer to "Did you notice a repeating sequence" is not recorded in either program (code).

Bearing. Report the recall against the simulated chance values and call the result sequence learning with awareness measured, not implicit learning. Record the participant's spoken answer. At n = 1 no implicit or explicit classification is defensible.

### Q7. What does the evidence say about the timing groups, the tones, the musical experience question, the two-hand layout and custom sequences?

- Timing groups. The evidence conflicts: order learned to a similar degree with random, constant and phase-locked intervals, and learned timing only sped responses (Shin 2008 [ABS]); spatial learning of 52 ms with timing correlated with the order against 14 ms when not (Shin and Ivry 2002, Results [FT, repository]); predictable timing greatly helped order learning (O'Reilly et al. 2008 [ABS]); pauses of varying length abolished learning (Stadler 1995 [META], via Schwarb and Schumacher [FT]); regular against irregular grouping of subsequences set the extent of learning (Stadler 1993 [ABS]); an inconsistent RSI did not harm learning, and a long one sometimes hid it (Willingham et al. 1997 [ABS]); presentation rate changed the RT measure (Frensch and Miner 1994 [ABS]). The cyclical pattern puts the long gaps before positions 4, 7 and 10, which would chunk the sequence into 3, 3 and 4 items (arithmetic). The post-test changes the timing for the cyclical and random groups (code).
- Tones. Location-informative tones gave faster RT reduction than uninformative ones under explicit instructions (Leow et al. 2025, Studies 2 and 3 [FT, preprint]); the authors limit the finding to conditions where explicit learning occurs (Limitations). Interference from a new auditory sequence had no credible effect while a new visual one did, which the authors read as learning tied to the visual-spatial cue with sound as an accessory (Leow et al. 2026, Discussion [FT]). The lab's files are E5 to A5, about 115 to 130 ms audible, and 10 to 12 dB quieter for squares 3 and 4 (arithmetic), where the published studies used 100 ms tones C4 to F4 at 65 dBA through headphones [FT].
- Musical experience. The script asks six categories by years before the task (script 59-66). The lab's 2026 study asked about formal training only at the end and analysed none against some; musicians were faster by 45.9 ms and anticipated more, with no credible difference in learning rate (Leow et al. 2026, Participants and Results [FT]).
- Two hands. The LRP is the contralateral minus ipsilateral activity over C3 and C4 relative to the responding hand, averaged over both hands (Li et al. 2017, Methods [FT]); SRT studies used it to show early preparation of expected responses (Eimer et al. 1996 [ABS]; Rüsseler and Rösler 2000 [ABS]). With one board, the two-hand layout runs only on the keyboard (D F J K) (code). The lab's latest EEG SRT used one hand on purpose, so that motor beta lateralises the same way for everyone (Lum et al. 2025, SRT task [FT]).
- Custom sequences are checked for all four squares, no repeats and 4 to 16 items (`sequence_problem` 116-143), not for counts or reversals, while the random blocks stay the same, so the structural differences of Q3 change with every custom sequence (code).

Bearing. The groups are the lab's own and should stay. Which way timing structure moves order learning is disputed in the literature, and a handful of participants cannot settle it. The tones are part of the lab's stimulus, and their benefit is shown only under explicit instructions.

### Q9. What do force pads change compared with the lab's keyboard?

- Detection: a pad press is the smoothed force crossing 30 percent of the way from rest to the calibrated light press; samples arrive in USB bursts about 20 ms apart and are stamped on arrival (`EEG_Lab/README.md`, Timing); press stamps run 10 to 11 ms late on a bursting board (SD about 6 ms) and the detector fires 7 to 11 ms after the raw crossing (`docs/research/new_modes/modes-review.md`, Device facts; design doc). A logged pad RT therefore sits about 17 to 22 ms after the force crossing, minus whatever the display lag is (arithmetic). Keyboards add their own switch travel and USB delay; Bridges et al. measured with a 1 kHz button box and warn that consumer keyboards are less precise [FT].
- Fingers. In choice tasks the edge fingers are fastest: the thumb and little finger beat the index, middle and ring fingers in a five-choice task (Lachnit and Pieper 1990 [ABS]), and the middle finger was about 100 ms slower than the thumb, index and little finger in a ten-choice task, with the ring finger in between (Wilimzig et al. 2012, Results [FT, PMC page]). Confusions fall on neighbouring fingers (Lachnit and Pieper [ABS]). On the pads, thresholds and finger strength add device effects. These load onto squares, which matters little for the plain effect (simulation, Q3) and about 10 ms for the cyclical group's matched effect if the little finger is slow (simulation).
- One hand against two. The lab's studies used the index and middle fingers of both hands on V B N M (Leow et al. 2025 and 2026, Procedure [FT]); one hand's four fingers bring in the ring and little fingers. In the pilot, two-handed keyboard play gave 5 wrong keys of 21 answered practice trials and one-handed play 1 of 14 (pilot).

Bearing. Pad RTs are their own scale. Absolute RTs and error rates from the pads cannot be compared with the lab's keyboard values; the sequence effect, preferably proportional, can be compared only as an order of magnitude.

### Q10. Does the notebook compute the right things?

### Q11. Instructions, screens, breaks, order and the hand

- The script asks for speed only; the lab's papers asked for speed and accuracy (Leow et al. 2025 and 2026, Procedure [FT]). The replication keeps the script's words, which is right for fidelity; a different instruction would change the error rate and the speed-accuracy setting (Q4).

- In order B of the Lab session the SRT follows four other games, about 9 minutes of play (Force Pilot 3.5, Chords 2.7, Buzz Hunt 2.4 and Adaptive 0.5 min, design doc Section 2.3) plus a 60 s stretch, including Buzz Hunt's sequence replay stage on the same fingers (code); the script ran as a standalone task.

### 6.2 Reliability and change expectations

- Retest of SRT learning scores pools near r = 0.28 (0.16 to 0.40); split-half near 0.63 (Oliveira et al. 2023 [FT]). All pooled retest studies used SOC material at 0 to 250 ms; no estimate exists for this design.
- One participant's sequence effect carries an interval of about ±26 to 37 ms from trial noise alone (simulation); a true 40 ms effect is usually visible in one person, a true 20 ms effect often not.
- A handful can show a group effect near 40 ms (power 0.83 at n = 5 with a between-person SD of 25 ms; 0.43 with 50 ms) (simulation).
- Timing-group differences need about 40 per group for a true d of 0.8 (arithmetic).
- No MDC, no individual change score and no trait claim for the SRT learning score.

### 6.3 Claims to avoid

- "Implicit learning", unless the verbal and recall data support it; this design favours awareness (Q6).
- "The response-to-stimulus interval is 500 ms" without "nominal"; the key-to-flash interval is about 530 to 550 ms at 60 Hz in both programs.
- "Identical to the laboratory's task" without naming the display, response device, hand layout and audio path.
- "Location tones improve sequence learning on this task", or citing Leow et al. 2025 for the structure of Nissen and Bullemer's experiment.
- That the timing groups or the musical experience question are the platform's additions.
- That dropping each block's first trial is the laboratory's rule.
- That reversals shorten RTs; that Nissen and Bullemer swapped random material in for the same people.
- That timing schedules do not change learning, as a settled finding.
- That the marker timing is validated before a light sensor and microphone have timed the flash and tone.
- Any EEG learning effect from one session beyond a single-person description; an LRP from one-hand pad play.
- Comparisons of pad RTs, error rates or anticipation rates with the laboratory's keyboard values.
- That d = 0.8 needs 26 per group "because" the retest correlation is 0.3.
- That after stroke results justify the healthy Lab session's design.

### Simulation and arithmetic assumptions (for Sections 1 to 6)

- Frame probe and tests: the repository's `tests/test_srt_mode.py` and `tests/test_mirror_srt_review.py` (90 tests) and a probe built on their `Sim` class, run on a clean `git archive` export of d48362f in a scratch folder with SDL's dummy drivers: a perfect flip clock at 1/60 and 1/120 s, the update 1 ms after each flip, keys pressed 300 to 450 ms after the due flash time, a short protocol (8 random trials either side, 2 learning blocks of 3 cycles), constant 500 ms. Outputs: flash frames per trial and logged `rsi_ms`.
- Response-to-flash interval: a press equally likely anywhere within a frame; at 60 Hz, 30 interval frames plus the flash flip; keys read at the start of the next frame loop (replication) or by `getKeys` before the next flip (script); pads delayed by 7 to 11 ms (detector) plus 0 to 20 ms (USB burst) before the loop sees them, from the design doc and `EEG_Lab/README.md`.
- Sequence structure: exact counts over the cyclic sequence; equivalence with Nissen and Bullemer's sequence checked over all 24 relabellings, 10 rotations and both directions.
- Random blocks: 20,000 blocks of 48 from the shipped `random_targets` with Python's `random.Random(7)`.
- Recall chance: 100,000 random 10-item recalls per model (uniform with repeats; uniform without immediate repeats), scored with the shipped `recall_scores`; partial knowledge as following the sequence's successor of the last two recalled squares with probability 0.3, 0.5, 0.8 or 1.0 (20,000 each), otherwise a random non-repeating square.
- Power and artefacts: per trial RT = 420 ms + normal(0, sigma) + exponential(tau) with (sigma, tau) = (40, 50) ms (SD about 64) or (50, 80) ms (SD about 94); block 8 = the sequence ten times, post-test = one shipped random block; 6 percent errors and 1 percent misses at random; the lab's exclusions (correct only, trial 1 out, 0 to 1,000 ms); medians; a true effect as a shift of block 8; one-sided Mann-Whitney U for one person (1,000 sessions per cell) and one-sample one-sided t on per-person effects for groups (600 cohorts per cell), between-person SD of the true effect 25 or 50 ms; bootstrap intervals from 600 resamples within blocks (200 sessions per cell). Artefacts: reversal cost 0, 20 or 37 ms added to trials whose square equals the one two back; finger offsets of +40 ms on square 4, or +20 ms on squares 2 and 3, or 0/15/30/10 ms on squares 1 to 4; no true learning. Matched effect: cyclical block 8 trials after a 500 ms interval (39 trials), 3,000 sessions per cell.
- Frequency-only learning: RT proportional to the information of each target given the learned location counts and the no-repeat rule (Hyman-type), at 25, 50 or 150 ms per bit; first-order learning: information under the sequence's first-order transition probabilities.
- Tone analysis: the four WAV files read with Python's `wave`, averaged over channels; fundamental and harmonics from a Hann-windowed FFT; RMS over the file; envelope from 1 ms RMS windows.
- Luminance: WCAG relative luminance of the theme's sRGB values and of PsychoPy's named colours, assuming an sRGB display; eccentricity from the logical layout scaled to a 52 cm wide display at 60 cm.
- Sample sizes: noncentral t and F (scipy), two-sided alpha 0.05, power 0.80.
