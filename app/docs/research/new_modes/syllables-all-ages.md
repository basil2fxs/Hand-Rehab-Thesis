## Status in the code, 25 September 2026

What was built from this review, and what was left as it was on purpose.

- **The study's block is unchanged.** Basil kept the healthy baseline battery exactly as pre-registered (Section 1.4, S6 and S7), so the battery pins a `classic` profile and nothing below reaches it. The one exception is a fix, not a design change: foils that sound like the target are no longer drawn (C4), in every profile.
- **Built:** the age profiles (C2) in `syllables_profiles.py`, chosen from the intake age: `6-9`, `10-12`, `13-15`, `16+` and `60+`, with the fall tables, the adult fall-only 4-down-1-up staircase and its threshold (C3), print fading (C6), foil shares with the new morphological F9 (C7), the sound lead for children (C8), plain adult presentation and larger tiles for 60 and over (C9), and one replay per set on R (C11). Teen and adult word pools and 60 made-up words are in `assets/words/syllables_pools.json` (C5), built by `scripts/build_syllables_pools.py`.
- **Audio (C1, section D):** Basil will record the voice. `scripts/syllables_recording_kit.py` writes the reading script with a spelling-pronunciation hint per chunk, cuts the recorded pages into files, levels them and writes the manifest; the game plays a recorded chunk for every word that holds it and stretches the model beat to fit. About 380 items for the adult pool, 1,860 for everything.

The split-policy change for the child bank's -er words (C10) and the stress step (C12) were not made: the first would change the study's own material, and the second belongs outside the battery.
- The notebook reads the new row fields (`prof`, `lex`, `print`, `replay`), keeps S1 to S7 on the child design, and summarises the older profiles apart (`sec_syllables_profiles`).

## How sources were checked

- PubMed web pages returned a captcha to the fetch tool, so PubMed-indexed papers were checked through the Europe PMC REST API, which mirrors PubMed and returns the PMID, DOI and abstract.
- Other papers: Crossref DOI metadata (often with the abstract), ERIC records, publisher, university or conference archive pages. Where only a secondary summary of an abstract could be read, the entry says so.
- Licences were read from the licence file or the OSF licence record, except where marked.
- Nothing was downloaded or installed and no project file was changed. Figures about the code and logs come from reading the repository (read-only).

## Facts from the code and logs that the review relies on

1. **Bank.** 692 words (band A 293, B 187, C 212): 368 two-syllable, 223 three-syllable, 101 four-syllable. 1,809 syllable tokens, 788 unique chunks. 1,117 of the 1,809 tokens (62 percent) are not the stressed syllable of their word, so most option sets target a syllable whose vowel is normally reduced in natural speech.
2. **No age input.** Intake metadata already stores `age`, but the mode never reads it. All material is child vocabulary, there are no pseudowords, and `MAX_SYLLABLES = 4`.
3. **Timing.** Fall table 4.0 to 2.5 s, with the floor `MIN_FALL_S = 2.5` hard-coded as a class constant. MODEL beat 500 ms. ATTEND 1.5 s.
4. **Print before the choice.** During ATTEND and MODEL the whole word is printed large and each chunk is printed in its slot as it is spoken. During CHOOSE the word is hidden and only answered chunks show.
5. **Speech.** Files from `assets/speech` (empty), else macOS `say` reading the chunk's spelling, else silence. The lab PC runs Windows, so the battery block is silent unless files ship. The mixer runs at 44.1 kHz with a 512-sample buffer; speech plays on one channel, so a new file cuts off the one before. The renderer writes 22.05 kHz files.
6. **Battery override.** Rung 3 start (fall 3.5 s, respeak on), 12 words, rounds of 6, 10 s break, band A, `seed: null`, speech backend `auto`.
7. **Foil legality checks spelling, not sound.** I re-ran the foil generator in memory over the bank (5 draws per word position and kind) with a crude non-rhotic Australian English sound key. It flagged foils that sound like the target in about 6.7 percent of vowel foils on unstressed syllables and 1.4 percent on stressed ones, for example ter to tir or tur (after, battery). The onset generator also makes c/k swaps (cap to kap, cat to kat) and the coda generator makes double-letter swaps (bas to bass, cal to call, cel to cell). These are pseudohomophones, the F8 class that is meant to be off by default. The key is rough and over-flags some items (ture to tire), so treat the figures as an estimate.
8. **Returns cannot be switched off in config.** `return_after` falls back to `[2, 4]` when given an empty list.
9. **Developer bench logs** (`sessions/2026-09-23` to `2026-09-25`, five Syllables blocks, macOS `say` fallback, age blank, one marked abandoned). 44 option sets: 32 answered after the prompt buzz, 6 answered unaided, 1 missed, 5 from an older build without the class field. The rung stayed at 1 on 43 of 44 sets, because prompted answers do not move the staircase. Median time to a correct press was 3,413 ms with the buzz (which fires at 2.4 s) and 1,845 ms without. These are tests, not data. They show that a player can wait for the buzz and hold the block at the easiest rung while `first_press_accuracy` reads near 100 percent.

## A. Verified reference list

Format: citation; identifiers; study type and sample; finding in my own words; how verified. Codes (L1, I3 and so on) are used in Sections B to F.

### A1. Dyslexia across the lifespan

- **L1** Reis A, Araújo S, Morais IS, Faísca L (2020). Reading and reading-related skills in adults with dyslexia from different orthographic systems: a review and meta-analysis. *Annals of Dyslexia* 70(3), 339-368. DOI 10.1007/s11881-020-00205-x; PMID 32918699. Meta-analysis, 178 studies, adults with dyslexia vs matched controls. Very large deficits on reading and writing tasks (d about 1.7 to 2.0), moderate on comprehension (0.73); speed measures worse than accuracy for word and pseudoword reading, phonological awareness and orthographic knowledge; smaller deficits in transparent orthographies. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L2** Swanson HL, Hsieh C-J (2009). Reading disabilities in adults: a selective meta-analysis of the literature. *Review of Educational Research* 79(4), 1362-1390. DOI 10.3102/0034654309350931. Meta-analysis, 52 studies, 776 effect sizes. Overall adult deficit 0.72, moderate to high on naming speed, phonological processing, verbal memory, word attack and spelling; deficits persist across age. Verified: Crossref abstract.
- **L3** Herrmann JA, Matyas T, Pratt C (2006). Meta-analysis of the nonword reading deficit in specific reading disorder. *Dyslexia* 12(3), 195-221. DOI 10.1002/dys.324; PMID 17009769. Meta-analysis, 34 reading-level-matched studies, N = 2,865. Moderate nonword deficit (d = 0.65), smaller with passage-level tests and in older samples. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L4** van IJzendoorn MH, Bus AG (1994). Meta-analytic confirmation of the nonword reading deficit in developmental dyslexia. *Reading Research Quarterly* 29(3), 266-275. DOI 10.2307/747877. Meta-analysis, N = 1,183. Modest deficit vs reading-level controls (d = 0.48), under 6 percent of variance. Verified: Crossref metadata; effect size from the Leiden repository summary.
- **L5** Van den Broeck W, Geudens A (2012). Old and new ways to study characteristics of reading disability: the case of the nonword-reading deficit. *Cognitive Psychology* 65(3), 414-456. ERIC EJ976702. Methods review with data. Argues the nonword deficit vanishes under better controls and that the core problem is building word-specific spelling knowledge. Verified: ERIC and ScienceDirect listing (abstract via secondary summary).
- **L6** Bruck M (1992). Persistence of dyslexics' phonological awareness deficits. *Developmental Psychology* 28(5), 874-886. DOI 10.1037/0012-1649.28.5.874. Dyslexic children, adults with childhood diagnoses, age and reading-level controls. Onset-rime awareness catches up; phoneme awareness does not, even in adults with fair word recognition. Verified: Crossref metadata; UCLA abstract page.
- **L7** Bruck M (1993). Component spelling skills of college students with childhood diagnoses of dyslexia. *Learning Disability Quarterly* 16(3), 171-184. DOI 10.2307/1511325. College students vs college and Grade 6 controls. Their spelling problems traced mainly to weak sound-spelling mapping knowledge. Verified: SAGE listing; abstract via search.
- **L8** Shaywitz SE, Fletcher JM, Holahan JM, et al (1999). Persistence of dyslexia: the Connecticut Longitudinal Study at adolescence. *Pediatrics* 104(6), 1351-1359. DOI 10.1542/peds.104.6.1351; PMID 10585988. Cohort followed from kindergarten to Grade 9. Persistently poor readers did not catch up; reading rate and spelling best separated average from struggling adolescents. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L9** Nergård-Nilssen T, Hulme C (2014). Developmental dyslexia in adults: behavioural manifestations and cognitive correlates. *Dyslexia* 20(3), 191-207. DOI 10.1002/dys.1477; PMID 24842581. 26 Norwegian adults with dyslexia vs 47 controls. Spelling was the clearest adult marker, then text reading fluency; RAN predicted fluency and nonword reading. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L10** Callens M, Tops W, Brysbaert M (2012). Cognitive profile of students who enter higher education with an indication of dyslexia. *PLoS ONE* 7(6), e38081. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0038081; PMID 22719864. 100 vs 100 Dutch-speaking students. Reading and writing deficits d 1 to 2, phonological d above 0.7; larger on speed than accuracy, except spelling. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L11** Greenberg D, Ehri LC, Perin D (1997). Are word-reading processes the same or different in adult literacy students and third-fifth graders matched for reading level? *Journal of Educational Psychology* 89(2), 262-275. DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.89.2.262. 72 adult literacy students vs 72 reading-level-matched children. Adults were weaker on phoneme segmentation and nonword reading and stronger on sight-word tasks. Verified: Crossref metadata; findings via secondary reviews of adult basic education research.
- **L12** Catts HW, Compton D, Tomblin JB, Bridges MS (2012). Prevalence and nature of late-emerging poor readers. *Journal of Educational Psychology* 104(1). DOI 10.1037/a0025323; PMID 24273341. Longitudinal cohort. 13.4 percent became poor readers only in later grades; of these, 36 percent had word-reading problems alone and 12 percent had word reading plus comprehension problems. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L13** Kairaluoma L, Torppa M, Westerholm J, Ahonen T, Aro M (2013). The nature of and factors related to reading difficulties among adolescents in a transparent orthography. *Scientific Studies of Reading* 17(5), 315-332. DOI 10.1080/10888438.2012.701257. 80 poor vs 189 typical Finnish adolescents. Problems showed in speed and in timed pseudoword accuracy. Verified: Crossref metadata; abstract via search.
- **L14** Ziegler JC, Perry C, Ma-Wyatt A, Ladner D, Schulte-Körne G (2003). Developmental dyslexia in different languages: language-specific or universal? *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* 86(3), 169-193. DOI 10.1016/S0022-0965(03)00139-5; PMID 14559203. 149 English- and German-speaking children. In both languages: slow reading, a nonword deficit larger than the word deficit, and slow serial decoding. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L15** Egan J, Tainturier M-J (2011). Inflectional spelling deficits in developmental dyslexia. *Cortex* 47(10), 1179-1196. DOI 10.1016/j.cortex.2011.05.013; PMID 21745662. 28 dyslexic 9 year olds vs age and spelling-age controls. Specific trouble spelling the -ed inflection and less use of morphological relations. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L16** Treiman R, Gordon J, Boada R, Peterson RL, Pennington BF (2014). Statistical learning, letter reversals, and reading. *Scientific Studies of Reading* 18(6), 383-394. DOI 10.1080/10888438.2013.873937; PMID 25642131. 130 children aged 5 to 6. Reversals were common, followed letter statistics and did not predict later reading. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L17** Brooks AD, Berninger VW, Abbott RD (2011). Letter naming and letter writing reversals in children with dyslexia: momentary inefficiency in the phonological and orthographic loops of working memory. *Developmental Neuropsychology* 36(7), 847-868. DOI 10.1080/87565641.2011.606401; PMID 21978009. Family genetics sample, mean age 11. Few children reversed at all; those with dyslexia reversed more; reversals are not a core feature. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **L18** Fowler CA, Liberman IY, Shankweiler D (1977). On interpreting the error pattern in beginning reading. *Language and Speech* 20(2), 162-173. DOI 10.1177/002383097702000208. Beginning readers. Final consonants were misread more often than initial ones; consonant errors were phonetically close to the target, vowel errors were not. Verified: Crossref abstract.

### A2. Interventions by age

- **I1** Hall C, Dahl-Leonard K, Cho E, et al (2023). Forty years of reading intervention research for elementary students with or at risk for dyslexia: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Reading Research Quarterly* 58(2), 285-312. DOI 10.1002/rrq.477. 53 studies, 6,053 students, K to Grade 5. Overall g = 0.33; larger for word reading and spelling than comprehension; more dosage, larger effects; grade and multisensory instruction did not moderate. Verified: Crossref abstract.
- **I2** Scammacca NK, Roberts G, Vaughn S, Stuebing KK (2015). A meta-analysis of interventions for struggling readers in Grades 4-12: 1980-2011. *Journal of Learning Disabilities* 48(4), 369-390. DOI 10.1177/0022219413504995; PMID 24092916. 82 studies. Mean effect 0.49 overall but 0.21 on standardised measures; effects have fallen as designs improved. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **I3** Bhattacharya A, Ehri LC (2004), already in the existing note. Re-verified detail: *J Learn Disabil* 37(4), 331-348, DOI 10.1177/00222194040370040501, PMID 15493405; the benefit of graphosyllabic analysis was largest for students reading at about Grade 3 level and smaller for stronger readers. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **I4** Diliberto JA, Beattie JR, Flowers CP, Algozzine RF (2009). Effects of teaching syllable skills instruction on reading achievement in struggling middle school readers. *Literacy Research and Instruction* 48(1), 14-27 (online 2008). DOI 10.1080/19388070802226253; ERIC EJ822147. Quasi-experimental, middle-school students with high-incidence disabilities or at risk. Syllable types, division and accent, practised on nonsense and low-frequency words; treatment beat control on word identification and comprehension. Verified: Crossref metadata; ERIC summary.
- **I5** Toste JR, Capin P, Vaughn S, Roberts GJ, Kearns DM (2017). Multisyllabic word-reading instruction with and without motivational beliefs training for struggling readers in the upper elementary grades: a pilot investigation. *Elementary School Journal* 117(4), 593-615. DOI 10.1086/691684. RCT pilot, 59 struggling readers in Grades 3 to 4, 24 sessions. Both multisyllabic groups beat control on word-reading fluency. Verified: Crossref metadata; ERIC summary.
- **I6** Toste JR, Capin P, Williams KJ, Cho E, Vaughn S (2019). Replication of an experimental study investigating the efficacy of a multisyllabic word reading intervention with and without motivational beliefs training for struggling readers. *Journal of Learning Disabilities* 52(1), 45-58. DOI 10.1177/0022219418775114. RCT replication, n = 109. Both multisyllabic conditions beat controls on reading and comprehension. Verified: Crossref abstract.
- **I7** Heikkilä R, Aro M, Närhi V, Westerholm J, Ahonen T (2013). Does training in syllable recognition improve reading speed? A computer-based trial with poor readers from second and third grade. *Scientific Studies of Reading* 17(6), 398-414. DOI 10.1080/10888438.2012.753452; ERIC EJ1023155. Computer training, 150 Finnish poor readers. Trained syllables got faster; transfer to word reading appeared only for long, infrequent syllables. Verified: Crossref metadata; ERIC abstract.
- **I8** Müller B, Richter T, Karageorgos P, Krawietz S, Ennemoser M (2017), already in the existing note. Re-verified detail: RCT, 75 German Grade 4 poor readers, 16 sessions built on the 500 most frequent syllables; strong effect on single-word recognition fluency, none on text comprehension. *Frontiers in Psychology* 8, 1635; PMID 28979233. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **I9** Ecalle J, Magnan A, Calmus C (2009). Lasting effects on literacy skills with a computer-assisted learning using syllabic units in low-progress readers. *Computers & Education* 52(3), 554-561. DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2008.10.010. French low-progress readers, syllable-highlighting software vs whole-word software. Short and medium-term gains in phonemic awareness, decoding and word reading for the poorest readers. Verified: Crossref metadata; abstract via secondary summary.
- **I10** Kyle F, Kujala J, Richardson U, Lyytinen H, Goswami U (2013). Assessing the effectiveness of two theoretically motivated computer-assisted reading interventions in the United Kingdom: GG Rime and GG Phoneme. *Reading Research Quarterly* 48(1), 61-76. DOI 10.1002/rrq.038. Poor readers aged 6 to 7, 12 weeks, vs untreated controls. Both GraphoGame versions improved reading, spelling and phonological skills, held at follow-up. Verified: Crossref abstract.
- **I11** Goodwin AP, Ahn S (2010). A meta-analysis of morphological interventions: effects on literacy achievement of children with literacy difficulties. *Annals of Dyslexia* 60(2), 183-208. DOI 10.1007/s11881-010-0041-x; PMID 20799003. 17 studies. d = 0.33 overall; phonological awareness 0.49, morphological awareness 0.40, vocabulary 0.40, comprehension 0.24, spelling 0.20. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **I12** Goodwin AP, Ahn S (2013). A meta-analysis of morphological interventions in English: effects on literacy outcomes for school-age children. *Scientific Studies of Reading* 17(4), 257-285. DOI 10.1080/10888438.2012.689791; ERIC EJ1012349. 30 studies. d = 0.32 overall; decoding 0.59, phonological awareness 0.48, morphological knowledge 0.44, vocabulary 0.34, spelling 0.30; no effect on comprehension or fluency. Verified: Crossref metadata; abstract figures via search.
- **I13** Bowers PN, Kirby JR, Deacon SH (2010). The effects of morphological instruction on literacy skills: a systematic review of the literature. *Review of Educational Research* 80(2), 144-179. DOI 10.3102/0034654309359353. 22 studies, preschool to Grade 8. Benefits, especially for less able readers; no less effective for younger students; better combined with other literacy teaching. Verified: Crossref abstract.
- **I14** Gray SH, Ehri LC, Locke JL (2018). Morpho-phonemic analysis boosts word reading for adult struggling readers. *Reading and Writing* 31(1), 75-98. DOI 10.1007/s11145-017-9774-9; PMID 29367806. RCT, adults studying for alternative high-school diplomas, 8 h tutoring on 40 academic words. Teaching morpheme and syllable structure beat whole-word study on reading untaught words (word attack and word recognition). Verified: Crossref metadata; abstract via search.
- **I15** Alamprese JA, MacArthur CA, Price C, Knight D (2011). Effects of a structured decoding curriculum on adult literacy learners' reading development. *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness* 4(2), 154-172. DOI 10.1080/19345747.2011.555294; PMID 22163055. Cluster RCT, 16 programs, 349 adults. Small effect on one decoding measure; none on word recognition, spelling, fluency or comprehension. Verified: Crossref metadata; abstract via search.
- **I16** Sabatini JP, Shore J, Holtzman S, Scarborough HS (2011). Relative effectiveness of reading intervention programs for adults with low literacy. *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness* 4(2), 118-133. DOI 10.1080/19345747.2011.555290. Randomised (compromised), 300 adults, 148 completers, three child-derived decoding and fluency programs. No program beat another. Verified: Crossref metadata; What Works Clearinghouse study page.
- **I17** Kindl J, Lenhard W (2023). A meta-analysis on the effectiveness of functional literacy interventions for adults. *Educational Research Review* 41, 100569. DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2023.100569. Three-level meta-analysis of controlled studies in five languages. Small pooled effect, g = 0.22 (95 percent CI 0.12 to 0.32). Verified: Crossref metadata; figures via search results.
- **I18** Nshimbi JC, Serpell R, Westerholm J (2020). Using a phone-based learning tool as an instructional resource for initial literacy learning in rural African families. *South African Journal of Childhood Education* 10(1), 620. DOI 10.4102/sajce.v10i1.620; ERIC EJ1251185. GraphoGame with Grade 2 children and 37 parents (mean age 36) in Zambia. Children beat controls; parents improved from pre to post. Shows adults can use the format, not that it works for them. Verified: ERIC abstract; Crossref.
- **I19** Steacy LM, Elleman AM, Lovett MW, Compton DL (2016). Exploring differential effects across two decoding treatments on item-level transfer in children with significant word reading difficulties. *Scientific Studies of Reading* 20(4), 283-295. DOI 10.1080/10888438.2016.1178267; PMID 28596701. RCT, 37 children in Grades 3 to 6, 60 lessons. The program that taught variant vowel pronunciations and larger units transferred better to words with variant vowels. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **I20** Strong GK, Torgerson CJ, Torgerson D, Hulme C (2011). A systematic meta-analytic review of evidence for the effectiveness of the 'Fast ForWord' language intervention program. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* 52(3), 224-235. DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7610.2010.02329.x; PMID 20950285. 6 trials. Training with acoustically modified (slowed and stretched) speech had no effect on any outcome. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **I21** Breznitz Z, Shaul S, Horowitz-Kraus T, Sela I, Nevat M, Karni A (2013). Enhanced reading by training with imposed time constraint in typical and dyslexic adults. *Nature Communications* 4, 1486. DOI 10.1038/ncomms2488; PMID 23403586. Training study, typical and dyslexic adults. Reading under a shrinking time limit shortened reading times and improved comprehension, held at 6 months; the same training without the limit did not help. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.
- **I22** Wood SG, Moxley JH, Tighe EL, Wagner RK (2018). Does use of text-to-speech and related read-aloud tools improve reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities? A meta-analysis. *Journal of Learning Disabilities* 51(1), 73-84. DOI 10.1177/0022219416688170; PMID 28112580. Small positive effect on comprehension (d = 0.35). Cited only as evidence that synthetic speech is an accepted reading support. Verified: Europe PMC abstract.

## B. Current design vs evidence

Verdicts: **Supported** (direct evidence fits), **Partly** (fits with a caveat, or the evidence is indirect), **Not supported** (evidence points the other way, or the design fails the task logic for this group), **No evidence** (nothing found either way).

**B1 Audio.** Speech is the stimulus the task depends on: the letter-sound binding the mode claims to train happens in auditory cortex and needs the sound (V1, V2, V5). Three problems. (a) The lab PC has no audio, so on Windows the block is a print task. (b) A system voice reading a lone spelling is unpredictable, and children understand synthetic words less well than adults, with single words the worst case (S10, S11); dyslexic listeners lose most when speech is degraded (S16). (c) The design does not say which form a chunk should take. 62 percent of targets are unstressed syllables, normally reduced (S3), and syllables cut out of running speech are poorly identified (S2). If a chunk is played as it sounds inside the word (for example the "tor" of doctor as a schwa), the vowel foils tar, ter and tur sound the same as the target and can only be told apart by spelling knowledge, which is the F8 construct the mode keeps off by default. A spelling pronunciation of the chunk avoids this, and has its own evidence base (S5 to S9; see D).

**B2 Print before the choice.** For young children, seeing and hearing the chunk together is a reasonable teaching step (V9; graphosyllabic analysis uses print, I3). But at CHOOSE the child may answer from memory of the print just shown rather than from the sound, so the unaided score mixes sound-to-print binding with short-term memory for print. Adults hold two to four printed chunks with ease, so for them the block can be done without listening at all. Support that helps novices can hinder skilled learners (F8).

**B3 Word bank.** The bank fits young children: familiar, imageable, Australian words and one split chosen by the spoken boundary (Kearns 2020). For older readers the gaps are derived words, low-frequency words and pseudowords. Multisyllabic and morphological teaching is what helps struggling adolescents and adults (I4 to I6, I11 to I14), root knowledge drives reading of long derived words (W12), and nonwords are the standard way to measure decoding apart from word knowledge (L3, L4, W16, W17). Adult literacy learners lean on sight words while their nonword reading lags (L11). Healthy adults will be fast and accurate on familiar child words, with only a syllable-count effect to separate items (W11), and some items (teddy, puppy, lolly) read as childish. One data issue: -er words are split two ways, at the morpheme (scoot-er, toast-er, train-er) or at the spoken boundary (far-mer, tea-cher, dri-ver). That is harmless for young children but a mixed signal for older readers.

**B4 Bands.** The 8 of 10 rule over 10-word windows moves slowly, which suits children (no direct evidence for the thresholds). In a 12-word battery block the band can move at most once and the block starts at band A, the easiest child words.

**B5 Syllable count.** Four syllables is a sensible cap for young children (span argument in the existing note). The span argument does not apply to adults, who meet five-syllable words often and pay a steady cost per syllable (W11).

**B6 Foils.**
- F1 far foils: fine at the child entry rung (learning the task); for adults they are distractors almost nobody picks (O3).
- F2 onset: supported for children (Bruck and Treiman, existing note). Easy for adults: word-initial consonants are the least error-prone position (L18). The generator's c/k swaps are pseudohomophones (Facts, item 7).
- F3 vowel: supported at all ages in principle (vowel spellings are the least consistent part of English, existing note; teaching variant vowels transfers, I19; adults' spelling problems trace to sound-spelling mappings, L7). Not supported on reduced syllables unless chunks are spoken as spelling pronunciations, and some swaps are Australian English homophones (ter, tir, tur).
- F4 reversal: common in all 5 to 6 year olds and not predictive (L16); more common in 10 year olds with reading difficulty (Terepocki 2002); few 11 year olds reverse at all (L17). Useful up to about 9 or 10, of little value for adults.
- F5 transposition: letter position dyslexia is real but uncommon (Kohnen 2012); low weight at every age.
- F6 other syllable of the word: tracks position for children; trivial for adults with the strip on screen.
- F7 coda: supported for children and adolescents (final consonants are misread more than initial ones, L18). The generator's double-letter swaps (bas to bass) are pseudohomophones.
- F8 pseudohomophone, off by default: right for adults, because adults who see misspellings, including as test distractors, spell worse afterwards (O7, O8), and multiple-choice lures get learned (O5), though feedback reduces this (O6). 10 year olds were not harmed in one study (O9).
- Missing for 10 and over: morphological foils (I11 to I14, L15). Missing at every age: a sound check that guarantees one defensible answer (O2).

**B7 Four options.** Four fits the hardware, sits inside GraphoGame's range and gives a flat 25 percent chance line. Item-writing research finds three good options are enough and a fourth is often picked by under 5 percent of people (O1, O3), so the analysis should report which foil kinds actually draw errors.

**B8 Staircase.** 3-down-1-up is standard (T6, T9) and matches GraphoGame's target of about 80 percent. Caveats: a fixed equal-step staircase settles away from its nominal point in forced choice (T7); the 85 percent optimum is theory for binary classifiers (T10); children played longer when a game was easier (T11). The rung also moves three things at once (foil similarity, fall time, respeak), so the final rung is not a clean threshold. And because prompted answers do not move it, a player who waits for the buzz holds rung 1 (bench logs: 32 of 44 sets prompted, rung 1 on 43 of 44).

**B9 Fall time.** Right for 6 to 9 year olds, but the docstring's figure needs correcting: Hale (T1) puts children at 1.8 times adult time at age 10 and 1.5 times at 12, so "1.5 to 1.8 at age 8" describes 10 to 12 year olds, and 8 year olds are slower still (T2, T3). A 15 year old is as fast as an adult (T1), so the 2.5 s floor is loose for teenagers. For adults it adds no time pressure (bench unaided median 1.8 s at rung 1, six sets, tests only), even though speed is where adult dyslexia shows most (L1, L10). Adults over 60 need more time again (T4, T5).

**B10 Prompt buzz.** The rationale (try first, then help that fades) is sound for children, but the evidence for time-delay prompting comes from other populations (existing note). The floor based on the child's own median time only switches on after three unaided correct answers, so it never engages for a player who always waits. In the adult battery the buzz reveals the answer, prompted answers leave the delay ladder where it is, and on the bench most sets waited for it.

**B11 Feedback and rewards.** The timing (immediate positive, quiet wrong press, late correction, spaced returns) is well supported for children (existing note). For adults, timing matters less once the lag to test is equal (F6), and delayed feedback helps on multiple-choice learning (F5), so the current timing suits every age. Games help learning more than motivation (F2), and gamification's gains are mainly cognitive (F1). Stickers and "You built the whole word!" have no evidence for adults; guidance for adult learners stresses relevance, respect and near goals (F7).

**B12 Typography.** Extra spacing helped dyslexic children in Zorzi 2012 and explains the Dyslexie font's gain (F9), but gave no benefit to Dutch Grade 2 and 4 readers naming words (F10); among adults with dyslexia only crowding-prone readers gain (F11). A moderate 0.12 tracking is a reasonable middle. Older adults need a larger x-height (F12).

**B14 The battery block as a measure.** Silent on Windows, child words from band A, prompt waiting that holds the rung, and a fall floor far above adult RT: first-press accuracy will sit near 100 percent with little spread between people. The rung can move at most about 8 times in the roughly 26 sets that 12 child words produce.

## C. Recommended changes, ranked by value

Each change lists what to do, the parameters, the evidence and the risk to the healthy-adult study. Code touch points are named so the changes stay within this code base.

### C1. Put real speech on the lab PC, recorded as spelling-pronunciation chunks plus natural words

- **What.** Fill `assets/speech` with (a) one natural recording per word and (b) one recording per unique chunk, spoken as a careful spelling pronunciation and reused in every word that contains it (788 chunks for the current bank). Set `speech.backend: file` in the battery override so a missing file is logged, never replaced by `say`. Ship the folder in the Windows build and check it on the lab PC before the first participant. Speaking rules and the recording protocol are in Section D.
- **Parameters.** 44.1 kHz, 16-bit, mono WAV (the mixer's own rate, so no resampling). Lead-in silence under 10 ms. Chunk files under 450 ms, or set the MODEL beat to max(500 ms, longest chunk + 150 ms), because a new file cuts off the previous one on the single speech channel. Chunks equalised to one RMS level (within 1 dB); words to one loudness (BS.1770, R12). New manifest fields: `speaker`, `accent: en-AU`, `chunk_form: spelling`, `recorded_on`, `latency_ms`.
- **Evidence.** S2, S3, S5 to S11, S14 to S16, V1 to V5.
- **Risk to the study.** None to timing. It is what makes the block measure sound-to-print matching. If files cannot be ready in time, either report the block under an honest name (print-chunk recognition after a modelled display) or drop it from the battery to protect session time, and say which in the methods.

### C2. Add an age band to the mode, with a frozen adult profile for the battery

- **What.** New key `syllables.age_band: auto | 6-9 | 10-15 | 16+ | 60+`. `auto` parses the intake `age` that metadata already holds. A blank age gives `16+` inside the study battery preset and `6-9` elsewhere. Each band selects the word pool, syllable range, pseudoword share, foil weights, fall table and floor (make `MIN_FALL_S` a profile value), staircase rule, MODEL print, prompt, spaced returns, sound timing and reward style. Log `age_band` and `profile_id` on every row.
- **Parameters.**

| Syllables | 2 to 3 (4 in band C) | 2 to 4 | 3 to 5 | 3 to 4 |
| Pseudowords | 0 to 10 percent | 20 percent | 30 to 50 percent (battery 50) | 30 percent |
| Fall table, rungs 1 to 8 (s) | 4.0, 4.0, 3.5, 3.5, 3.0, 3.0, 2.5, 2.5; floor 2.5 | 10 to 12: 3.2, 3.2, 2.8, 2.8, 2.4, 2.4, 2.0, 2.0; floor 2.0. 13 to 15: 2.8, 2.8, 2.4, 2.4, 2.0, 2.0, 1.7, 1.6; floor 1.6 | Fall-only staircase (C3): start 2.4, bounds 1.0 to 3.0 | 13 to 15 table; floor 1.6 |

- **Evidence.** Fall tables: 10 year olds about 1.8 times adult time, 12 year olds 1.5, 15 year olds about 1.0, choice RT slowing again through adulthood (T1 to T5). Staircase: T6 to T8. Sound timing: V2 to V4, V8. MODEL: F8, B2. Prompt: bench logs. The table values are my scaling of those ratios onto the existing child table, not measured values for this task.
- **Code touch points.** `syllables.py` (profile loading, `MIN_FALL_S`, returns switch, since an empty `return_after` currently falls back to `[2, 4]`), `syllables_words.py` (pools, `MAX_SYLLABLES` per band), `syllables_foils.py` (weights), `config/default.yaml` (profiles and the battery override).
- **Risk to the study.** This is the change that makes the battery block informative. Freeze the adult profile before the first participant and do not change it mid-collection. Session length: a 12-word adult block runs about 2 to 3 minutes (per word: 1.5 s attend, about 3.5 sets of roughly 1.3 s answer plus 0.7 s hold and gap, longer on misses, 1.4 s complete, 0.8 s gap; plus one 10 s break). That is similar to the current block: longer words add sets, and dropping MODEL and the faster fall take the time back. Use a fixed `seed` in the battery so every participant gets the same words, foils and lanes in the same order. With a fall-only staircase and returns and ease-in draws off, the random draws no longer depend on performance, so the item sequence is identical for everyone and item-level comparison becomes possible.

### C3. In the battery, report a speed threshold and unaided accuracy, not first-press accuracy

- **What.** Primary adult outcome: the time threshold, the mean fall time over the last 12 sets of the fall-only staircase (also the mean of the last 6 reversal points when at least 6 occur). Secondary: unaided first-press accuracy, median correct RT measured from speech onset, and errors captured per foil kind. Keep `first_press_accuracy` in `block_stats`, but label it as including prompted sets, as it does today.
- **Parameters.** Foil mix fixed at the adult weights (C7), independent of the staircase. After an error or a miss, fall +0.20 s; after 4 consecutive unaided correct sets, fall -0.17 s. The 0.17 to 0.20 ratio (0.85) is close to García-Pérez's recommended 0.8415 for 4-down-1-up (T7), and the rule targets 84.1 percent (T6), near the 85 percent learning optimum (T10). About 40 sets per block will give few reversals, so present the threshold as coarse, with its SD across people.
- **Evidence.** L1 and L10 (speed is the adult marker), T6 to T10, W10 (reliable individual differences between adults).
- **Risk.** A new metric with no norms. Comparability across participants is fine if everyone runs the same frozen profile.

### C4. Make foil legality sound-aware and move sound-alike swaps into F8

- **What.** Give every chunk a spelling-pronunciation key (IPA or a simple Australian English respelling) and reject any foil whose key equals the target's, whatever kind produced it. Until the key exists, block these swaps in F2, F3 and F7: c/k before a, o or u; single vs double final consonant (s/ss, l/ll, f/ff); vowel swaps inside one Australian English sound set (er, ir, ur, and ear before a consonant; or, aw, au; ee, ea; ai, ay; oi, oy). Log `hom=1` if a check had to be bypassed.
- **Evidence.** O2 (one defensible answer), O7 and O8 (adults learn misspellings they see), S4 (non-rhotic Australian English), Facts item 7 (about 6.7 percent of vowel foils on unstressed syllables, plus c/k and double-letter cases).
- **Code touch point.** `is_legal` in `syllables_foils.py`.
- **Risk.** None to timing. Fewer candidate foils means more fallbacks, which are already logged by the kind actually produced.

### C5. Build age-appropriate word pools, with pseudowords

- **6 to 9.** Keep the current bank. Tag each word with Kuperman AoA (W1, through NORARE, CC BY) and a child frequency (CPB-Lex W7, or CYP-LEX 7 to 9 W6). Keep words with AoA at most 5 for 6 to 7 year olds and at most 7 for 8 to 9 year olds, with child Zipf at least 3.5. This is a design rule (words known in speech about two years before they are read), not a published cut-off. Pseudowords only as a short decoding check (0 to 10 percent), since meaning supports the step from decoded form to word (S7 to S9).
- **10 to 15.** Add 150 to 250 words from CYP-LEX bands 10 to 12 and 13+ (CC BY): Zipf 2.5 to 4.5, AoA 7 to 12, mostly 3 to 4 syllables, at least 40 percent derived (a real prefix or suffix, checked against MorphoLex W8 offline). Pseudowords 20 percent.
- **16 and over.** 120 to 200 real words with SUBTLEX-UK Zipf 1.5 to 3.5 or AoA 10 or later, 3 to 5 syllables, at least 50 percent derived or compound, plus 40 to 60 pseudowords for the battery pool.
- **Pseudowords.** Assemble 3 to 4 chunks from the bank's own inventory, using only chunks with one clear spelling pronunciation (W15). Reject anything listed in CMUdict (R1) or sounding like a CMUdict word. Stress the first syllable in two- and three-syllable items (S3); where a real suffix is used, stress by the suffix (P10, P11). Record each whole pseudoword naturally. Tell adults up front that some items are made up.
- **New fields per word.** `lex` (word or pseudo), `aoa`, `zipf`, `morph` (0 or 1), `split_type` (spoken or morph), and per chunk `reduced` (0 or 1). Log them per set.
- **Licences.** CC BY sources (CYP-LEX, NORARE) can ship derived values with attribution. Use NC-SA sources (SUBTLEX-UK, MorphoLex) offline to choose words; ship only the words and your own splits, and name the source in `LICENCE.txt`. Shipping their frequency values would bring the NC-SA terms with them. This is a reading of the licences, not legal advice.
- **Evidence.** L3, L4, L11, L14 (nonwords and length effects), W1 to W17, I11 to I14, P11.
- **Risk.** About 200 to 300 new recordings for the adult pool.

### C6. Fade print before the choice

- **What.** Children: rungs 1 to 3 as now; from rung 4, MODEL lights the slot and plays the chunk without showing its text, and ATTEND shows the printed word only at rungs 1 to 3. Adults: never print the word or its chunks before the choice; the word is heard. Log `model_print=0/1`.
- **Evidence.** V9 and I3 (print helps early learning), F8 and the guidance hypothesis in the existing note (fading support), task logic (B2).
- **Code touch point.** `_draw_word_strip` and the ATTEND and MODEL branch in `ui/syllables_screen.py`.
- **Risk.** Harder for children at the higher rungs; the staircase absorbs it.

### C7. Foil weights by age

- **6 to 9.** Keep the current rung schedule. Allow F3 on unstressed syllables only once chunk audio is a spelling pronunciation. Cap F4 at one slot per set.
- **10 to 15.** Share of foil slots: F3 30, F7 20, F9 (morphological, new) 20, F5 10, F2 10, F6 5, F4 5 percent. F1 only at rung 1.
- **16 and over.** F3 35, F9 25, F7 20, F5 10, F2 10 percent. F1, F4, F6 and F8 at 0.
- **New F9.** Swap a real affix for another real affix with a different spelling pronunciation (un/in, dis/mis, re/de, ment/ness, ful/less), only on chunks that are morphemes.
- These weights are design choices guided by the error literature (L7, L15 to L18, I19), not measured optima; report them as such and check them with foil capture rates, treating a kind that draws under 5 percent of errors where it appears as non-working (O3).
- **Risk.** None to timing.

### C8. Timing of sound and print, and logging real onsets

- **What.** Children: sound starts 150 to 200 ms after the chunk appears (in MODEL and at respeak). Adults: synchronous. Log when the file actually starts playing, not when it was requested. Measure output latency once per machine with a loopback recording and store it in the manifest. When respeak is on, compute RT from speech onset.
- **Evidence.** Adult integration is strongest near synchrony (V2, V3); 11 year olds integrate at a 200 ms letter lead (V4); the binding window is wider in younger people and less precise in dyslexia (V6 to V8). This is indirect: these are neural integration studies, not teaching trials.
- **Risk.** Small code change; no effect on session length.

### C9. Adult presentation

- **What.** `reward_style: adult`: no stickers, star animations or "You built the whole word!"; plain progress; an end summary (threshold, unaided accuracy, median time). Plain adult wording in the instructions, including that some words are made up. For 60 and over: tile x-height at least 0.5 degrees at the viewing distance (about 5 mm at 60 cm) and the slower fall table.
- **Evidence.** F7, F1, F2, F12, T4.
- **Risk.** None.

### C10. Split policy by age, and fix the mixed -er splits

- **What.** Keep spoken-boundary splits for 6 to 9. Add an optional `morph_split` for derived words used by 10 and over (teach-er, farm-er). Make the -er family consistent in the base bank.
- **Evidence.** W12, I11 to I14, P12, Kearns 2020.
- **Risk.** A few extra chunk recordings (teach, farm, er).

### C11. One replay per set

- **What.** A supervisor key (or a spare key for adults) replays the chunk once; log `replay=0/1`; exclude replayed sets from the speed threshold.
- **Evidence.** Learner-paced segments helped in multimedia learning (S19, indirect). No direct evidence on replay in reading games.
- **Risk.** None if replayed sets are excluded from the threshold.

### C12. Optional, not in the battery: a stress step for 10 and over

- **What.** After COMPLETE, ask which syllable was the strongest (press the finger under it), using the bank's stress index; log accuracy.
- **Evidence.** Stress awareness is weaker in dyslexia from childhood to adulthood (P2 to P5), while implicit stress knowledge may be intact (P6, P7); the causal role is contested (P1, P8, P9).
- **Risk.** Adds time; keep it out of the healthy-adult battery.

## D. Audio plan

### D1. Recorded voice or TTS

- **Human recording (recommended).** One adult speaker of general Australian English records everything. It gives full control over the chunk form, matches the accent the participants hear every day (unfamiliar accents cost young children most, S14, and slow adults in noise, S15), and raises no licence question once the speaker gives written permission to ship the recordings with the software. Natural speech remains the reference against which synthetic speech is judged (S10, S11).
- **Offline TTS, no credentials needed.** Piper with an en_GB voice is licence-clean (engine MIT or GPL-3.0; "cori" voice trained on public-domain audio, R10) but British, and no en_AU Piper voice exists. Reading lone syllables would need phoneme input, which I have not tested. Windows' own en-AU voices (James and Catherine, R11) could speak at run time on the lab PC with nothing redistributed, but they are older standard voices, and how they read single syllables and whether they honour SSML phoneme tags is untested (unverified).
- **Cloud neural TTS.** Google, Azure and Polly have en-AU voices but need accounts and credentials, so they are out of scope here. The evidence on neural TTS is mixed: one study found it less intelligible in noise than older concatenative TTS (S12); another found AI speech behaved like human speech in noise for younger and older adults (S13).
- **macOS `say`.** Development only. The project's own note records that Apple's licence does not allow shipping these recordings; I did not re-check the licence text.
- **Decision.** Record a human voice. Do the adult pool first (it is what the battery uses), then the child bank. Allow TTS only as a development placeholder, flagged in the manifest.

### D2. How each item is spoken

- **Whole word.** Natural, careful citation form with its normal stress and a falling pitch; no exaggeration; normal rate. Slowed or stretched speech has no evidence of benefit (I20), and older listeners cope worse with altered timing (S17).
- **Chunk.** A spelling pronunciation: the way a reader decodes that chunk on its own. Record it once per unique chunk and reuse it in every word. Rules for the speaker:
  1. A chunk that carries the word's stress: say it as it sounds in the word, isolated (the "ti" of ti-ger rhymes with "tie").
  2. An unstressed chunk: never a schwa, which has no letter. Give the vowel its letters spell: the short vowel in a closed chunk (pen, bit, dol); "ar" as in car; "er", "ir", "ur" as in her; "or" as in for. For an open chunk, use the vowel it has when that chunk is stressed elsewhere in the bank, otherwise the short vowel.
  3. Consonant plus -le chunks (tle, ble, ple): one consistent light form, "tul".
  4. Write the intended form (IPA or a respelling) next to every chunk in the bank before recording, and have a second listener check a 10 percent sample against it.
- **Why this form.** Syllables cut from running speech are poorly identified (S2); 62 percent of targets are unstressed and usually reduced (S3); spelling pronunciations help children and adults remember spellings, including schwa letters (S5, S6); and moving from a spelling pronunciation to the real word is a separate, trainable skill that predicts reading (S7 to S9). The game trains that step every time it plays the natural word after the chunks. It also keeps vowel foils answerable by sound (B1, C4).
- **Pseudowords.** Reuse the chunk files. Record each whole pseudoword naturally with the stress written in the list, and avoid chunks with ambiguous graphemes (W15).

### D3. Timing relative to print

- **MODEL (children).** Print first, sound 150 to 200 ms later. Beat = max(500 ms, longest chunk + 150 ms). Adults: no MODEL.
- **CHOOSE respeak.** Tiles appear, then the sound after 150 to 200 ms (children), or at tile onset (adults). RT runs from speech onset.
- **ATTEND.** Word sound at print onset for children; sound only for adults.
- **COMPLETE.** Always finish with the natural word, which pairs the parts with the whole (S7 to S9; parts then whole, S19).
- **Evidence and strength.** V2 to V4 and V8 support near-synchrony for adults and a small print lead for children, but they come from neural integration studies, not teaching trials. Report the values as design choices.

### D4. Recording protocol (no credentials; about one afternoon of reading)

1. **Speaker.** One adult with a general Australian accent, not unwell on the day, who gives written permission to ship the recordings with the software under the repository's licence.
2. **Room and microphone.** A quiet room with soft furnishings; a phone or USB microphone 15 to 20 cm from the mouth, slightly off-axis, with a pop filter; one session, same posture and distance throughout.
3. **Settings.** 44.1 kHz, 16-bit, mono WAV; speaking peaks around -6 dBFS.
4. **Lists.** Words, chunks and pseudowords in random order, which avoids list intonation. Each item read twice with a one-second pause. The chunk list shows the respelling and the IPA.
5. **Size.** Current bank: 692 words + 788 chunks = 1,480 items. Adult pool: about 150 words, 50 pseudowords and 250 new chunks. At about 4 s per pair of takes, roughly 2 hours of reading for everything, or 30 minutes for the adult pool alone.
6. **Editing.** Split on silence, keep the better take, trim to under 10 ms lead-in and about 50 ms tail, add 5 ms fades. Equalise chunk RMS within 1 dB, because (as I understand the standard) BS.1770's gated measure works on 400 ms blocks and most chunk files are shorter than that; set words to one integrated loudness (BS.1770, R12). True peak at most -1 dBFS.
7. **Checking.** A second adult listener identifies a 10 percent sample of chunks from sound alone, against their real four-option sets. Re-record any chunk identified correctly less than 90 percent of the time. Note the check in the manifest.
8. **Manifest.** Speaker code, accent, date, microphone, `chunk_form = spelling`, every file with its duration and RMS, and the measured output latency on the lab PC.

## E. Thesis notes (bullets, not prose)

**Background**
- Dyslexia persists into adulthood; in adults the largest gaps are on speed, spelling and nonword reading (L1, L2, L9, L10). Phoneme awareness deficits persist even when word reading is fair (L6). Some poor readers only emerge around Grades 4 to 5 (L12).
- Long words are a separate hurdle for older readers: stress, vowel reduction, less predictable spellings and morphology (P12, W12). Syllable-based print training helps struggling readers from primary school to adolescence (I3 to I9), and morphology teaching adds to it (I11 to I13), including for adults (I14).
- Adult literacy interventions have small average effects (I15 to I17), and GraphoGame-type games are weak in English on average (McTigue 2020), so no transfer claim is made.
- Prosodic and stress sensitivity is reduced in dyslexia from age 6 to 26 (P4), but its causal role is disputed (P7 to P9).

**Methods justification**
- Four options: fits the hardware, gives a 25 percent chance line, and three working distractors is the usual ceiling in item-writing research (O1, O3).
- Audio: one Australian English speaker; chunks as spelling pronunciations; natural words (S2 to S9, S14 to S16).
- Adult profile: fall-only 4-down-1-up staircase (T6 to T8), no prompt and no printed model (F8), pseudowords to measure decoding apart from vocabulary (L3, L4, W16, W17), fixed item order for all participants.
- Fall tables by age from speed development (T1 to T5). Correct the existing claim: children are about 1.8 times slower than adults at 10 and 1.5 times at 12 (T1), not "1.5 to 1.8 at age 8".
- Foil legality includes a sound check (O2, S4).

**Discussion**
- In healthy adults the block measures how fast a skilled reader matches a heard syllable to its spelling under a deadline. Speed is the dimension where adult dyslexia shows most (L1, L10), but nothing about dyslexia can be concluded from healthy data.
- Which foil kinds draw adults' errors (vowel and morphological foils are expected to) is descriptive and can guide later child work.
- The bench logs showed that a prompt which reveals the answer invites waiting; state how the adult profile removed it and why the child profile keeps a fading version.

**Limitations**
- Healthy adults only; no children or readers with dyslexia; no standardised reading measure; no control group; one session.
- Foil weights, fall tables and sound timings are design choices built from indirect evidence (neural integration studies, RT development), not validated for this task.
- Spelling-pronunciation audio is a teaching convention; the literature supports it for spelling memory and decoding, not for this game in particular.
- One Australian English speaker; results may not carry to other accents.
- Short block (12 words, about 40 sets), so the threshold is coarse, and its reliability is unknown (Syllables is not in the pass-2 retest core).

**Framing line**
- "Syllables is included as a feasibility measure of sound-to-print matching speed in healthy adults. It is not a dyslexia test or treatment; use with children and with readers with dyslexia is future work."

## F. Conflicts and gaps

**Where sources disagree**
1. **Nonword deficit.** A reliable deficit against reading-level controls (L3, L4) vs no specific deficit once the design is properly controlled, with the core problem being word-specific knowledge (L5). The deficit also shrinks with age (L3). Pseudowords are still the standard decoding measure (W16, W17); use them as a measure without claiming they isolate a single cause.
2. **Auditory and prosodic accounts.** Temporal sampling and rise time (P1 to P4) vs vague causal chains and inconsistent findings (P8, P9). Stress difficulties may be metalinguistic rather than perceptual (P7), and implicit stress regularity is intact in adults with dyslexia (P6).
3. **Time pressure.** Where dyslexic binding breaks down (T12), vs a trainable ingredient that improved adult reading (I21); extra time as an accommodation is unresolved (T13).
4. **Feedback timing.** Delayed is better (F6 in children, F5 in adults, Gabay 2021 in dyslexia) vs immediate is better in classroom studies (F4) vs no difference (O6, and F6 in adults once lag is equal).
5. **Target accuracy.** About 85 percent is optimal for learning (T10, theory for binary tasks) vs about 80 percent in GraphoGame vs easier games are played longer (T11). The child profile keeps 3-down-1-up (79.4 percent); moving children to 4-down-1-up (84.1 percent) is a defensible alternative.
6. **Letter spacing.** Helps (Zorzi 2012, F9) vs no benefit for word naming (F10) vs helps only crowding-prone readers (F11).
7. **Syllable versus morpheme units.** Syllable training helps (I3 to I9) while syllable division rules are unreliable in English (Kearns 2020); morphological units predict long-word reading in older children (W12). Hence spoken splits for young children and morph splits for older readers (C10).
8. **Synthetic speech.** Less intelligible to children (S10, S11); neural TTS worse in noise than older TTS (S12) vs AI speech behaving like human speech in noise (S13).
9. **Seeing misspellings.** Harms adults' later spelling (O7, O8), with lures learned from multiple-choice tests (O5) vs no measurable harm in 10 year olds (O9), and feedback reducing lure learning (O6).
10. **Syllable training transfer.** Transfer only for long, infrequent syllables (I7); gains in word fluency but not comprehension (I8); GraphoGame in English positive (I10) vs weak (Ahmed 2020, McTigue 2020).
11. **Audiovisual binding in dyslexia.** A wider binding window (V6) vs poorer simultaneity judgement with no difference in the speech integration window (V7).
12. **Adult interventions.** Small or null effects (I15 to I17) vs a clear benefit from morpho-phonemic teaching (I14).

**Gaps (nothing found)**
- No study of a syllable-to-print matching game with adults, with or without dyslexia.
- No evidence that a vibration prompt helps reading (already noted in the existing note).
- No study of the best sound-to-print timing for teaching, only neural integration work.
- No evidence on audio replay in reading games.
- No open Australian English pronouncing dictionary was identified in this search, so the sound check in C4 has to rely on hand-written keys or crude rules.
- Evidence on age-appropriate design for adult learners is mostly consensus guidance (F7), not trials.
- No norms or reliability data exist for this task at any age.
